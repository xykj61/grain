A guard with one plain rule is easy to read: a page is covered, or it stands outside. A guard that
offers one and then discards it is harder, because three parties end up believing the same false
thing. The author writes the declaration. A reviewer reads it and moves on. The instrument throws
it away. Every party acts in good faith, and the block stays uncovered.

## The block that had nothing it could say

`tools/fixtures/t/tutorial_output_scan.sh` proves that every output block in `docs-geode` still
prints what its page promises. It accepts two HTML comments above an output fence, and they answer
different questions:

- `<!-- selected: ... -->` -- this block quotes SOME lines of a longer report, so compare by
  containment in order rather than byte for byte.
- `<!-- volatile: ... -->` -- these figures MOVE as the tree grows, so report rather than gate.

The parser consumes both into separate variables, `sel` and `vol`, so a block may lawfully carry
both. The comparison disagreed. On a containment failure the selected branch went straight to
`drift`, leaving `vol` unread.

`docs-geode/demos/README.md` quotes two lines of the maintenance meter -- `clutter=43 fascia=57`,
against 44 and 56 today. Both properties hold at once: it is a selection of a longer report, and
its numbers are free by construction. It had one thing to say and two comments with which to say it, and the reading heard one.

The page already knew the lesson. Two sections above, about its room-bound count:

> *That archive count climbs through the day as ledger rows fold onto shelves... read the line as
> the day's reading rather than the tree's. Nothing here holds it still; the command is what tells
> you today's.*

## Why an ignored declaration is the worse shape

A missing declaration leaves a visible hole. Somebody reading the scan's header sees that moving
figures have nowhere to go, and either the page changes or the guard grows one.

An ignored declaration covers that hole while leaving it open. The header documents the comment, the
page writes it, a reviewer sees it in the diff and reads the block as handled -- and the reading
discards it. This is the unheard-guard shape moved inside one scan, where no roster and no tier can
reach it, because from outside the guard runs and answers.

## The repair, and the leg that proves it

An `elif [ -n "$vol" ]` stands ahead of the drift branch, the header states that the two
declarations compose, and four legs stand in the pen: the refusal planted undeclared, then lifted
by the comment alone, with the count and the drift reading each asserted. Removing the branch reds
three of the four.

The same lap taught the pen to tally its own legs, because `control=ok` reports only that the run
reached its last line, so a deleted leg and a passing leg read identically from the witness's side.
A peer seated that repair upstream within the hour, in the stronger form: the control prints
`control_cases` and `control_failed`, and the witness asserts the failure count rather than a
number typed in prose. Theirs shipped. The witness's own header had already regretted carrying a
count two short for a fortnight, taken from its assertion list rather than from the pen -- and a
number the pen emits stays true to the pen.

## The half that cost a day

The same lap built a second instrument, `port_registry`: every `const <name>port<name>: u16 = N;`
in living tracked Rye, grouped by declaring file, gating values claimed by two or more modules.
Measured 35 constants across 21 files, 31 distinct, one bound to zero, and three numbers -- 38495,
38496, 38497 -- each declared by two modules. Forty-four pen legs, two mutations bitten, green on
metal.

It is withdrawn, because a peer landed `port_band` two hours earlier and upstream. That guard reads
the same declarations, ratchets the same three double claims at three, names the same base-port
blind spot as `counting_bases`, and adds a seated band and lock coverage on top. It is strictly the
wider instrument and it was there first.

**The absence check was taken and it was honest.** `sh tools/fixtures/p/path_absence_scan.sh`
fetches the anointed remote before answering, and it read `commits_behind=0` with the path missing
from both sides. Two hours later, ten commits had landed and one of them was this.

So the baton's ABSENCE clause holds exactly what it claims: **it answers about a remote as that
remote stands when asked.** A fleet of eight allocates faster than that answer keeps. The
clause exists because a seat concluded a file was gone from stale local bytes; this is the same
wall met by a seat whose reading was fresh and whose *conclusion* aged.

## What is missing, said plainly

**Absence is checkable. Intent is not.** Nothing in this tree lets a ship say *I am building this*
before the file exists, so two ships reading the same card, the same ledger and the same open line
reach for the same work and neither can see the other until one of them pushes.

It happened twice in one day. The `${frozen.out}` split in `mantra_cli_record_witness.rish` was
made here and upstream within the same hour, by two hands reading one guard's red line. The
instrument above cost a day.

The shape a repair would take is small: a claim is a stamp, a seat, and one line naming what is
being built, written where the card is read and cleared when the work lands or the lap dies. What
it must NOT become is a lock -- a ship blocked behind a stale claim is worse than two ships
duplicating a guard, since the second wastes tokens and the first wastes a night. Whether the fleet
wants one is Keaton's.

## What this does not reach

Whether the other scans in this tree honor the declarations they offer. Three accept a comment of
some kind, and this lap read one of them.

*May every declaration a page is offered be a declaration something reads, and may two hands
reaching for one thing find each other before the second one finishes.*
