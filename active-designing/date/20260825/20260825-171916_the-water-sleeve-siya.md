## The reading, first

The fiber sleeve memo read a mill: a modest industrial property, net-leased to an operator whose revenue is public spending. **A headwaters basin offers a different tenant and a materially different shape, and the difference is worth stating before the similarity.**

A fibre mill sells into private industrial markets with a public preference layered on top. A water business sells almost entirely to public bodies: districts, towns, counties, and the agencies that fund them. On the five screens the elder harvest named for public-receivable businesses -- *share*, *durability*, *wages over rents*, *circulation over extraction*, and *ahimsa* -- a water business scores near the ceiling on the first two by construction. **Which is exactly why the last three deserve the closer read**, and why a water sleeve reads safer than it is: durability of revenue and durability of the *business* are separate properties, and a district contract that renews forever is worth precisely what its renewal terms say.

The short form: **the real-estate sleeve owns boring ground under a water-adjacent tenant; the private-business sleeve owns the laboratory or the service company; the venture sleeve owns the instrument and the ledger; and the public sleeve owns the listed engineering and recovery firms by role, screened.** Four hands, one basin, each rewarded for a different thing on purpose.

## What a water tenant rewards, sleeve by sleeve

**Real estate -- the ground and the roof.** A water business's real estate is unglamorous: a shop, a yard, a laboratory, a warehouse for pipe and pumps, near the districts it serves. Held by a syndicate in which Siya is one limited partner among several, net-leased. The property is boring on purpose, and in a valley where industrial space runs two to three times a Midwest metro's rate, the boring building is a large share of the deal's return rather than an afterthought. **Read the rent against the local rate before reading it against the tenant.**

**Private business -- the operating company.** An independent water laboratory, a monitoring and instrumentation service, or a small treatment-operations contractor. The Civic reading here is unusually clean and unusually demanding: **the whole civic value of an independent laboratory rests on its independence, and an owner who is also a landlord to the districts it tests for has quietly created the very conflict the laboratory exists to hold apart.** That is a structural finding rather than a caution, and it decides which combinations of these sleeves may be held at once.

**Venture -- the instrument and the ledger.** The signed-reading stack this tree already builds: a keypair per verifier, a signed event per reading, an append-only log, a fold anyone can run. Sold to districts, watershed groups, and the agencies that fund monitoring. Small, patient, and the piece where the family's own architecture is the product rather than an accessory.

**Public -- the listed firms, by role.** Water engineering, advanced treatment, nutrient recovery, and remediation. Held by role, screened by the five screens, sized inside the position law the Siya policy statement already carries. Counsel names no tickers here; a fresh screen belongs in its own dated study.

## The conflict rule, stated plainly

One rule governs every combination above, and it is the reason this memo exists rather than a copy of the fiber sleeve memo.

**Hold the verification and the operation apart, in different hands, permanently.**

Civic Style asks it of every programme: verification is done by people with a real stake in the truth and no stake in the result. A family fund owning the laboratory, the operating contractor, and the landlord's interest under the district's own building would pass every financial test and miss the only test that matters. The clean shapes are:

- own the **ledger and the instrument**, and sell them to everyone including competitors; or
- own the **operating company**, and buy verification from a laboratory nobody in the family touches; or
- own the **laboratory**, and hold no operating or landlord interest anywhere in the same basin.

Two of the three at once wants a written wall and an outside eye. All three at once is the one combination this memo sets aside.

## The gates, in order

Every one of these stands ahead of any money moving, and counsel names them rather than walks them.

1. **Securities counsel.** A syndication is a private securities offering, usually under Regulation D; the rule chosen decides who may participate and how the offering may be spoken about.
2. **The fee-only fiduciary and the Siya investment policy statement.** The sleeve percentages, the bands, the position law, and the forbidden moves live there; this memo reads inside that document and changes nothing in it.
3. **The Wyoming public-benefit charter.** Siya's own governance names its public benefit. A basin position that funds verification serves it; one that only collects a promote does not.
4. **Water counsel, which the fiber memo did not need.** A closed basin, a contested exempt-well doctrine, an adjudication still running through the water court, and a tenant whose value may rest on a water right make this a specialist's question from the first conversation rather than a later one.
5. **Entity separation.** Fund, property, operator, laboratory, and instrument each keep their own books from the first dollar, in Dimeroll, with inter-entity facts signed on both sides.
6. **Keaton's word.** Money, custody, keys, and wire vocabulary are stop-and-park triggers by standing law. Counsel proposes; the word seats.

## What the tree adds that a syndication usually lacks

Dimeroll keeps one set of books per entity, never braided, with any value crossing between them recorded as an explicit signed inter-entity fact on both sides. Linengrow's receipts carry the field, mill, and water facts in the same append-only shape.

Offered to co-limited-partners as a **glass ledger** -- the tenant's books and the basin's readings foldable by anyone holding the log -- that is a due-diligence surface no sponsor's quarterly letter matches. In a water deal it is worth more than in a mill deal, because the asset's whole thesis is that somebody's reading is trustworthy. **A fund that can prove its own numbers is the natural owner of the tooling that proves everyone else's**, and that is the honest reason the venture sleeve leads here while the real-estate sleeve follows.

## What this refuses

**It recommends no number.** Thirty percent is Keaton's stated reading; whether it fits the family's whole picture is the fiduciary's question. **It leaves the sponsor, the district, and the deal unchosen.** **It buys no water right**, which in a closed basin is a purchase with its own body of law and its own counsel. **Every dollar waits on the gates above and on the word.**

## Falsifiers

The sleeve reading wants revisiting if a candidate tenant's revenue proves to rest on a single annual appropriation rather than a multi-year contract or a statutory duty; if the local industrial rent makes the property's yield thinner than a plain index would return for the same exposure; if water counsel reads a tenant's water right as contested; or if the available structures all put verification and operation in one family's hands. Any one of those sends the memo back to the shapes.
