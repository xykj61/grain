## The reading, first

Keaton has named a sleeve: roughly 30% of the Siya Fund LLC portfolio into commercial real estate syndications as a limited partner beside other limited partners, pointed at properties whose tenants are ecological businesses with public-contract receivables and books the tree can read. This memo reads that sleeve the way Civic Style reads a policy -- name the outcome, name what the reward measures, keep the two aligned -- and shows where a Kansas City fiber corridor fits inside it. The short form: **the real-estate sleeve owns the mill's ground and roof and leases them to the operator; the venture sleeve owns the brand; the private-business sleeve may own the operator; the public sleeve owns listed firms in the same sectors, screened.** Four hands, one corridor, each rewarded for a different thing on purpose, so that a stumble in one is a lesson rather than a collapse in all four.

## What a syndication rewards, and how to keep it pointed at the good

A syndication is a group purchase. A sponsor -- the general partner -- finds, buys, and runs the property; limited partners supply most of the money and receive most of the return after a preferred return, with a share of the upside to the sponsor as a promote. Three parties, three rewards:

- **The sponsor's promote** rewards deal volume and the paper rate of return. It points at the good when the sponsor has its own money in the deal at a share large enough to hurt, the fees are named in basis points, and the preferred return is real rather than decorative.
- **The limited partner's return** rewards cash yield and preservation. It points at the good when the property is boring, the tenant is durable, and the debt is modest and fixed.
- **The tenant's lease** rewards whatever the rent formula rewards. A fixed net lease rewards the tenant for staying solvent; a revenue-share lease rewards the tenant for revenue, which is closer to the corridor's real outcome and harder to verify.

**The alignment, plainly.** A fibre-mill property is worth its lease, the lease is worth its tenant, and the tenant is worth its receivables. So the real-estate sleeve is underwriting the operator's revenue one step removed, and the honest move is to read that revenue against the five screens the elder harvest named for public-receivable businesses: *share* of revenue that is public, *durability* of the spending (a statute or a specification rather than a one-year line), *wages over rents*, *circulation over extraction*, and *ahimsa*. A mill selling hurd to builders on a state specification and bast to erosion-control makers on a DOT approved list scores well on share and durability once the specifications are on the record, and scores on the last three by construction of the corridor.

**What the tree adds that a syndication usually lacks.** Dimeroll keeps one set of books per entity, never braided: the fund's, the property's, and the operator's, with any value crossing between them as an explicit signed inter-entity fact recorded on both sides. Linengrow's receipts carry the field and mill facts in the same append-only shape. Offered to co-limited-partners as a glass ledger -- the tenant's books and the corridor's receipts foldable by anyone with the log -- this is a due-diligence surface no sponsor's quarterly letter can match, and it is the thing that would make other limited partners want to sit in this deal beside Siya rather than beside a stranger.

## The gates, in order

Every one of these stands ahead of any money moving, and counsel names them rather than walks them.

1. **Securities counsel.** A syndication is a private securities offering, usually under Regulation D; the rule chosen decides whether limited partners must be accredited and how the offering may be spoken about. The three-hats papering the roster discipline already keeps -- user, investor, compensated spokesperson each documented separately -- applies with full force the moment a musician on the roster is also a limited partner or a brand voice.
2. **The fee-only fiduciary and the Siya investment policy statement.** The sleeve percentages, the bands around them, the position-size law, and the forbidden moves live in that document; this memo reads inside it and changes nothing in it.
3. **The Wyoming public-benefit charter.** Siya's own governance names its public benefit; a syndication that serves the corridor serves it, and one that serves only a promote does not.
4. **Entity separation.** Fund, property, operator, and brand each keep their own books from the first dollar, in Dimeroll, with inter-entity facts signed on both sides.
5. **Keaton's word.** Money, custody, keys, and wire vocabulary are stop-and-park triggers by standing law. Counsel proposes; the word seats.

## How the corridor fits, sleeve by sleeve

**Real estate (the ground and the roof).** A rural-county industrial site with outdoor bale storage, a modest building, and road access to the metro's rail hub, held by a syndicate in which Siya is one limited partner among several, net-leased to the mill operator on a fixed rent with a modest revenue-share kicker. The property is boring on purpose. If the operator stumbles, the building still stands on the second-tightest industrial market in the Midwest, and a bale barn re-leases.

**Venture (the brand).** A first capsule on partner-spun cloth, cut in the metro, sold on a receipt, inside the venture sleeve's position-size law. The brand is where the value blooms and where the risk is honest; it is held apart from the property so that a brand that takes longer than hoped costs the family a venture check rather than a building.

**Private business (the operator).** Either a stake in the Lexington processor that already runs a one-ton-an-hour line, or a new operating company that contracts its idle hours -- decided after the three quotes and one afternoon the place study names, and after the operator's first season of books can be read.

**Public (the listed firms).** The sector list Keaton pasted -- closed-loop battery metallurgy, direct lithium extraction, advanced water reclamation, waste-to-fuel, struvite recovery, biochar for remediation, low-carbon cement, aggregates -- reads as firms whose revenue is public spending. Held in the public sleeve by role and screened by the five screens, they are the liquid end of the same thesis the corridor makes in soil: **public money pointed at the public good, verified by someone with no stake.** Counsel names no tickers here; the elder harvest already did, and a fresh screen belongs in its own dated study.

## What this refuses

**It recommends no number.** Thirty percent is Keaton's stated reading; whether it is right is the fiduciary's question, read against the family's whole picture. **It picks no sponsor and no deal.** A syndicate is chosen on a named sponsor's audited record, co-investment, fee load, and preferred return, and on an appraisal and condition report from people with no stake. **It moves nothing.** Every dollar waits on the gates above and on the word.

## Falsifiers

The sleeve reading is wrong if a first-season operator's books show public receivables under a fifth of revenue with no specification on the record; if a candidate sponsor's own money in the deal is under a share that would hurt; or if the fiduciary reads the family's liquidity and finds a real-estate sleeve of this size leaves too little cash for the years a mill and a brand take. Any one of those sends the memo back to the shapes.
