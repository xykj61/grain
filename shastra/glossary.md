# Glossary -- One Term, One Plain Meaning

**Where this sits:** home is [`README.md`](README.md)
**Style:** Bhakta (see `../context/BHAKTA_STYLE.md`) -- no term below assumes you already know
another one on this page
**Last updated:** 2026-09-18

---

Single-stranded, silo-style: one Sanskrit term per entry, one plain-language meaning, no entry
leaning on a later one to make sense. Read top to bottom the first time; after that, look up
whatever a study file sends you here for.

## pramana

A **means of valid knowledge** -- literally, "that which measures." Classical Indian philosophy
asks, for any claim, *how do we know this is true?* and answers with a short list of accepted
pramanas: direct perception, inference, and scriptural testimony (sabda) are the three every
school of Vaishnava thought accepts. Jiva Goswami's whole *Tattva Sandarbha* is an argument about
which pramana settles questions the other two cannot reach -- and it answers: scripture, when the
question is about what lies beyond the senses and beyond inference's reach.

## tattva

**Truth, or a category of what-is** -- not "a fact" in the small sense, but the reality a thing
actually is beneath how it appears. *Tattva Sandarbha* studies which pramana can tell us tattva at
all, before any later Sandarbha tries to say what that truth contains.

## sambandha, abhidheya, prayojana

Three words that together answer *what is a scripture actually doing?* -- and Jiva Goswami reads
the whole Bhagavata Purana through this one lens:

- **sambandha** -- the relationship. Who am I, what is the world, what is the Absolute, and how do
  the three actually stand toward each other? A scripture's sambandha teaching tells you where you
  really are.
- **abhidheya** -- the practice. Once the relationship is known, what does a person actually *do*?
  This is where bhakti -- devotional practice -- enters as the answer.
- **prayojana** -- the goal. What is practice *for*? The Gaudiya answer is prema, love, not merely
  liberation from suffering.

The Six Sandarbhas divide along exactly this line: the first four settle sambandha, the fifth is
abhidheya, the sixth is prayojana.

## sandarbha

Simply **a systematic treatise or arrangement** -- not a mystical word, a working one. Jiva
Goswami's six are called Sandarbhas because each arranges (sam-, "together") a body of scriptural
verses (-darbha, from a root meaning "to gather" or "to weave") into one continuous argument,
rather than a random collection of quotations.

## rasa

Literally **taste, or savor** -- in Sanskrit poetics generally, the aesthetic flavor a well-made
work of art evokes in someone who can receive it. Gaudiya theology extends the same word to
describe the different flavors of relationship a soul can have with the divine -- reverence,
friendship, parental affection, and romantic love each named as a distinct rasa. *Priti Sandarbha*
is where Jiva Goswami works this out in full.

## prema

**Love**, in its fullest and most mature sense -- not affection as a passing feeling, but love as
the very point of existence, the prayojana every sandhana of scripture is finally in service of.
Where bhakti (below) names the path, prema names the fruit that path is grown to bear.

## bhakti

**Devotion, as a discipline and a practice** -- not a mood one happens to feel, but something a
person actually *does*, cultivated the way any skill is cultivated. *Bhakti Sandarbha* is Jiva
Goswami's most practical work: what devotion looks like in daily life, and what makes a practice
count as bhakti rather than something that merely resembles it.

## Uttara Veda

Not a fixed technical term with one settled scholarly definition -- rather, the theological claim
this room's [`uttara-veda/README.md`](uttara-veda/README.md) exists to study in full: that the
Bhagavata Purana functions as the Veda's own **later, riper word** (uttara = later, higher), the
fruit the Vedic tree was always growing toward. The word choice echoes **Uttara Mimamsa**, the
classical name for Vedanta philosophy as the "later inquiry" following Purva Mimamsa's ritual
concerns -- and the echo is not an accident. Jiva Goswami's argument is that the Bhagavatam
completes what Vedanta only pointed toward.
