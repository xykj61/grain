## The finding, first

**Hemp is the crop, the Missouri River counties east of the metro are the ground, and the mill should start at one ton an hour beside people who already run one.** Kansas City sits on the temperate-continental row of the foundation's own table, which grows hemp; its summers are too hot and its springs too short for textile-grade flax at scale, so linen enters as a spring trial rather than the lead. Field retting works on this ground and puts water first without a tank. The binding constraint is straw under contract, then spinning, and only then capital -- the same finding the earlier South American study reached, now read on Missouri numbers. The public doors are real and mostly free: a no-cost federal licence, federal crop insurance in most Missouri counties since 2024, a state grant precedent for exactly this equipment, and a new federal definition of hemp that names fibre and grain as the plant's protected future. The value blooms in the brand, and the brand meets the mill at a receipt.

## 1. The ground

Kansas City is where the Kansas River meets the Missouri. The metro straddles two states and sits on deep river-bottom alluvium along the Missouri and loess uplands on either side. The climate is humid continental: about 39 to 40 inches (1,000 mm) of rain a year, about 190 frost-free days, a July mean high near 89 F (32 C), and a last spring frost in mid-April (long-run normals, 1991-2020, national weather service; verify at the airport station page before a planting calendar is written).

Missouri ranked second only to Kentucky in national hemp output in the mid-to-late 1800s, grown for cordage and canvas along this same river (university extension, MX77). The state trialled nineteen hemp cultivars at Sikeston in 2022 and published the multi-location results in February 2026, which gives a grower a cultivar list read on Missouri weather rather than on a seed catalogue.

## 2. The town

Three layers of self-governing town touch this corridor: the federal government, which licences the crop and buys on specification; the State of Missouri, which has funded fibre processing once already; and the metro's cities and counties, which zone the mill and write the water permit.

```kyri
format fiber-town-v1
polis                  United States -- State of Missouri -- Lafayette, Ray, Jackson, Clay counties -- Kansas City
crop_licence           USDA hemp licence, no fee, rolling applications (Missouri under the federal plan since 2023-01-01)
water_permit           Missouri Department of Natural Resources; outfall applies only if a closed-loop tank is built
procurement_door       state DOT erosion-control and geotextile specifications; federal biobased purchasing; DoD domestic textiles
procurement_rewards    a measured spec (thermal, tensile, biobased share) -- or a certificate; read each one
public_money_door      USDA crop insurance (most Missouri counties, from the 2024 crop year); state fibre-processing grants (2021 precedent); USDA marketing grants (2024 precedent, Pennsylvania flax)
public_money_rewards   insurance rewards proven yield history; the state grant rewarded equipment purchase with a 25% match; the marketing grant rewarded a supply chain plan
zoning_for_mill        light-industrial with outdoor bale storage; the metro's rail hub is the asset, a county siding is the question
industrial_rent        USD 5.43 per sq ft per year, metro asking average, Q2 2026 (a national brokerage's quarterly figures)
falsifier              a state-level rule that re-licences fibre hemp, or a federal definition that moves against fibre
```

**The town's rules, dated.** Missouri closed its own hemp programme on 31 December 2022 and handed licensing to the federal Domestic Hemp Production Program, where a licence costs nothing and applications roll through the year. Federal enforcement of the rule that every hemp lot be tested by a DEA-registered laboratory is delayed to 31 December 2026. Federal crop insurance for hemp grown for fibre or grain reached most Missouri counties with the 2024 crop year. In 2021 the state appropriated USD 700,000 for hemp fibre processing equipment and awarded four reimbursement grants of up to USD 200,000 each with a 25% cash match; one of the four went to a processor at Lexington, forty miles east of the metro on the river.

**The definition of hemp changed under the crop.** Section 781 of the 2026 continuing appropriations act (P.L. 119-37) narrows the federal definition to a total-THC standard and caps finished cannabinoid products at 0.4 mg of THC per container, effective 12 November 2026; the Senate passed a thirty-day delay to 11 December on 8 August 2026 and the House returns on 31 August. The same section explicitly includes **industrial hemp** -- the stalk, the fibre, the grain, the seed oil -- as the protected use. Read the Civic way: the town has just told the market which half of the plant it rewards. The cannabinoid half contracts; the fibre half is named as the future. A structural signal rather than a cyclical one, with one cyclical risk attached: if cannabinoid acreage falls fast, the drying and decortication capacity it shared with fibre can thin before fibre acreage grows into it.

**The metro's real estate.** Kansas City's industrial market read 4.5% vacant in the second quarter of 2026 with a metro asking rent of USD 5.43 per square foot per year, among the three tightest of the thirty largest U.S. industrial markets, and one of the most affordable, against a national average asking rent above USD 10 per square foot. A 40,000-square-foot mill building at the metro asking rate is roughly USD 217,000 a year; a rural site in Lafayette or Ray County is likely lower, and that number is a named gap until a broker prices one.

One neighbour already runs the equipment. That is the first door to knock on, ahead of any equipment catalogue: a first lap in this valley is most honestly a *second* line beside a first, or a contract for the first line's idle hours, before it is a new building.

## 4. The corridor, with its named gaps

```kyri
format fiber-corridor-v1
field_to_mill_km       under 80 -- Lafayette, Ray, Carroll, Saline, Jackson, Clay
haul_mode              road first; the metro's rail hub for finished streams
first_buyers           hurd -> building infill and animal bedding; bast -> nonwovens, erosion control, composites
partner_spinning       named gap -- the largest U.S. decorticator announced in May 2026 that it spins certified American hemp into yarn in India, which says plainly where domestic spinning stands
brand_home             cut and sewn in the metro, on partner-spun fabric, until domestic spinning exists
return_path            compost for undyed and plant-dyed goods; take-back for the rest
falsifier              a bast lot with no offtake letter before the second season
```

The gap is the same one the whole country carries. In 2024 U.S. farmers harvested roughly 60.4 million pounds of fibre hemp from 18,855 acres, and the capacity to separate bast from hurd at industrial scale stayed far behind the harvest (market analysis, 2025). The country's largest line, at ten tons an hour in Texas, began commercial operation in April 2024 and now sends fibre abroad to be spun. The best-established U.S. hemp apparel house cuts and sews in Los Angeles on globally sourced fabric and names *seed to shirt* as its long-term goal precisely because the domestic spinning link is still to be built. A Kansas City corridor is honest about this from its first day: the mill sells three streams to industrial buyers, and the brand buys partner-spun fabric until the missing link exists.

## 5. Retting in a Kansas City summer

Spring-sown hemp cut at early flowering falls in late July or August here, into the hottest and stormiest part of the year. Field retting on dew and thunderstorm rain runs fast in that weather, which is good for speed and hard on judgement: a wet fortnight over-rets a windrow.

```kyri
format fiber-retting-v1
method                 field
days_expected          14-35, region-specific; the timer logs the truth
turn_count             1-2
moisture_floor_pct     15, with the reading taken at the bale
timer_home             linengrow/retting_timer.rye
liquor_treatment       none -- no water is drawn
outfall_bod_mg_l       none -- no outfall
nutrient_return        yes, by construction
falsifier              over-retting two seasons running moves the cut earlier or the harvest into September
```

The tree already ships the tool for this: a bounded timer with `start <name> <days>` and `status`, READY rising when the days complete, every refusal named. Add a moisture field at baling and a soil reading at the field edge and the batch log is a water receipt anyone can fold. And the numbers say why the field is the vat: untreated water-retting liquor has measured an average biological oxygen demand near 1,460 mg/L (semi-industrial flax basins, published 2018), several times untreated household sewage, and open-water retting has been abandoned across most of the world for that reason. On this ground the river stays a river.

**What the grower sees.** The university extension's 2025 fibre budget assumes 6,500 pounds of baled fibre straw per acre at USD 0.15 per pound, for USD 975 of income against USD 731 of total cost, leaving USD 244 per acre over total costs (October 2024 prices). That budget buys conventional fertility -- 100 pounds of nitrogen, 60 of phosphate, 90 of potash -- at USD 145 per acre. A plant-raised fertility programme (cover crops, green manure, the rotation itself) replaces that line with seed and one more pass, which counsel reads as USD 60 to 120 per acre (a planning range without a Missouri source), and it carries a yield question that only a trial plot answers. The falsifier is a two-season veganic strip beside a conventional strip on the same field, with the timer and the soil scientist watching both.

**What the mill sees, in the first lap.**

The arithmetic says what the earlier study said: a first lap selling raw streams runs thin to modest, proves the corridor, and earns the brand. Every figure above is a planning band, and the next lap is three papers -- a straw contract, a bast offtake letter, and a hurd offtake quote -- which turn this table into a budget.

**Second and third laps** follow the shape: two or three tons an hour on two shifts with cleaning and grading, fed by 3,000 to 4,000 acres and a team of 25 to 30; and the ten-ton-an-hour scale, which needs a state's worth of acres and is a decade horizon here, as it is everywhere.

## 7. The streams and their first buyers

Hurd goes first and closest: building infill (the hempcrete-type wall the foundation describes), bedding, and absorbents, sold by the ton to makers who can be reached by truck. Bast goes to nonwovens, erosion-control blankets, and composites -- industrial buyers who read a grade sheet rather than a fashion season. Dust is a byproduct with a small bedding and fuel market. Textile-grade bast is the third market and the last to open, because it waits on degumming and spinning that the country is still building; until then the corridor's garments are cut here on partner-spun fabric, and the receipt says so plainly.

## 9. Hemp or linen?

Hemp. The ground says so first: the foundation's table puts a warm-summer, real-winter climate on the hemp row, and Missouri's own trials, budget, insurance, and grant precedent are all hemp. Fibre flax wants a cool, damp spring and is pulled before summer heat; on this ground that is a March sowing and a late-June pull, which is possible and marginal for textile grade. The U.S. linen revival is real and young -- a first fibre-flax mill in more than fifty years in Oregon, a Pennsylvania field-to-fibre project with a federal award, and a North American trade association formed in 2022 -- and all of it is in cooler country than this. So linen enters here as a two-to-five-acre spring trial with those teachers named, and the corridor's name keeps both fibres in it on purpose.

## 10. The brand, read by role

Four houses show four ways to own the destiny of a garment, and each is named plainly here because the external-research room allows it.

- **The made-at-origin house** (Industry of All Nations, Los Angeles): garments made where the material grows, with natural dyes, shipped from the country of origin. Its reward is provenance the wearer can trace.
- **The chemical-free direct house** (Rawganique): hemp, linen, and organic cotton made in its own ateliers, sold direct, with process purity as the promise. Its reward is trust in the process.
- **The domestic basics house** (Jungmaven): hemp tees cut and sewn in Los Angeles, more than 95% U.S.-made by its own account, 100% hemp tees at USD 84 to 120 (2026), and *seed to shirt* as the stated horizon. Its reward is a daily garment and a domestic supply chain built one link at a time.
- **The one-collection atelier** (Jan-Jan Van Essche, Antwerp): natural fibres, one collection a year, garments made to last decades. Its reward is permanence.

The house this corridor would build takes provenance from the first, process purity from the second, the domestic basics and the patience from the third, and permanence from the fourth -- and adds the one thing none of the four can yet show: a receipt from the field, through the timer, the bale, the mill, and the sewing floor, that anyone can fold without trusting the brand. The first capsule is a few thousand pieces on partner-spun 100% hemp cloth, cut in the metro, plant-dyed or left undyed, priced beside the domestic basics house, and sold on a receipt.

## 11. Projections

**Within two seasons, a first lap can run.** Horizon: two growing seasons from the first grower meeting. Assumptions: ~700 to 1,250 acres under contract, an existing line's idle hours or a second line beside it, and offtake letters for hurd and bast. Falsifier: fewer than 400 acres contracted by the second spring. Confidence: plausible.

**Textile-grade domestic spinning arrives after the mill, and from elsewhere.** Horizon: five to eight years. Assumptions: the largest domestic decorticator keeps spinning abroad; the fibre carve-out holds; a domestic degumming and spinning line is built by someone larger than this corridor. Falsifier: a U.S. hemp spinning line commissioned within three years, which would move this projection up. Confidence: likely.

**The fibre carve-out is structural.** Horizon: through the next farm bill. Assumptions: section 781's industrial-hemp definition survives the delay bills unchanged in its fibre and grain clauses. Falsifier: a farm bill that re-licences or re-taxes fibre hemp. Confidence: likely, with the cyclical risk that shared processing capacity thins as cannabinoid acreage falls.

## 12. Base rates, before the story

Most first mills in a young fibre industry run below nameplate for their first two seasons, and the ones that close usually close for want of straw or buyers rather than for want of a machine. The country harvested about 3,200 pounds of fibre per acre on average in 2024 against the extension budget's 6,500-pound planning yield, so a grower's second season is where the acre count gets honest. The linen revival's oldest project is a decade old and still building its first mill. None of this is a reason to wait; all of it is a reason to contract the acres before the line and to sell three streams before one.

## 13. Structural and cyclical, sorted

Structural: the climate row, the river-bottom soil, the metro's rail hub, the free federal licence, the fibre carve-out in the definition, the domestic-textile statute, and the fact that a fibre crop asks less fertility than a grain. Cyclical: the metro's industrial rent (tight now, historically among the cheapest), the state grant (a precedent rather than a programme), the low-carbon federal materials preference (uncertain in 2026), and the cannabinoid contraction's effect on shared drying and decortication capacity.
