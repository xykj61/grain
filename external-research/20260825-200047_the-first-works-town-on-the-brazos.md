## The finding, first

**Plainview, Texas, is the first works town.** It is the seat of Hale County, incorporated in 1907, a home-rule city of about 20,000 people run by a council and a city manager. Its water reclamation plant stands beside Running Water Draw and returns about 1.8 million gallons a day against a 3.3 million-gallon permit, which makes the city the first steady return on the Salt Fork thread of the Brazos, the second of the two threads the Brazos study named. Below that return lie the White River, the reservoir that supplies four small towns' drinking water, the Salt Fork, and the whole Brazos to the Gulf. The commons a first lap wants are already standing: soccer fields on an 80-acre park laid out along the draw itself, a 1925 theatre the city owns downtown, a streetscape and reinvestment zone nearing completion, and a university that has grown with the town since 1908. And the city drinks from a reservoir a hundred miles north that a small salt works at Logan has been sweetening since 2001, so the town already lives inside the lesson this season's studies teach.

Post, the Garza County seat between the two forks, is the second seat: smaller, warmer, and shrinking, with a theatre that produces eight shows a year, a founder who built a cotton mill and tried to make rain with dynamite, and a water supply that reads Plainview's return as its own upstream ledger. Graham, the home-rule seat of Young County above Possum Kingdom, is the third. Granbury and Glen Rose carry the strongest commons on the river and stand at the wrong end of it for a works. Clovis, at the mapped source, remains the campus the Brazos study already read, with its window still open. Marlin is the hardest municipal case on the river and belongs to a later lap. The mouth pays for every reach above it.

The ranking of moves inside the first works town agrees with the Gallatin study's ranking for the Missouri: **the receipt first, the reuse lease second, the retrofit third, and a new plant nowhere**, because the plants that matter on the upper Brazos are built and running.

## 1. The three axes, read as rewards

Civic Style asks of every design what the reward measures. The maintainer named three axes, and each rewards something plain.

**Headwater rewards the whole river.** A receipt at the top is inherited by every intake below it. On this river the honest headwaters are outfalls rather than the mapped source, because the draws on the plain run a few days a year and the towns' returns run every day. The rungs that reward a works are therefore the first towns on each thread whose return runs year-round: Lubbock on the North Fork, Plainview on the Salt Fork thread. That is the same finding the Gallatin study reached for the Missouri, arrived at from a different plain.

**Polis rewards a signature that holds.** A syndicate signs with a council. An incorporated city can approve a services agreement in one meeting. A county seat gathers the courthouse, the district court, the hospital, and the civic calendar around one square. A home-rule city, which Texas law opens to a town of 5,000 or more that adopts a charter, carries powers a general-law town lacks, and usually a city manager who reads a proposal on its merits. The axis rewards a place where the person across the table can say yes and mean it.

**Commons rewards a town people stay in.** Soccer fields, a stage, a theatre, and a market are how a town proves it is worth a decade. A first lap wants these standing or seeded rather than promised, because a syndicate arriving with a stage and a plant in one meeting is asking for two kinds of trust at once.

**The tie rule.** Headwater first, polis second, commons third. A river's position is fixed and a town's bones can be grown, so the axis that stays put decides the tie. Under that rule Plainview leads, because it is the only upper-basin town that reads standing on all three.

## 3. Incorporated, county seat, home rule -- the three questions, answered

For the lean, all three answers are yes: Plainview is **incorporated** (1907), it is the **county seat** of Hale County, and it is a **home-rule** city under a council-manager form, with a city manager, a mayor, an economic development director, and an assistant city manager speaking for it on the record in February 2026. For the second seat, Post is incorporated (1914), the county seat of Garza County, and council-manager in form, and it stays **general-law** because its 4,790 people at the 2020 census sit under the 5,000 that home rule asks. For the third, Graham is incorporated, the Young County seat, and home rule. Of the alternates, Granbury is all three; Glen Rose is a general-law county seat; Clovis is a home-rule county seat under New Mexico's commission-manager form; Marlin is a home-rule county seat whose plant is the county's open wound.

## 4. The plant on the draw, and what a retrofit would change

The city's water reclamation plant treats an average of 1.8 million gallons a day, produces 2,306 tons of sludge a year, and watches seven lift stations outside its fence (city page, 2026). Its state discharge permit, renewed in 2004, authorizes up to 3.3 million gallons a day of treated domestic wastewater at a site beside Running Water Draw about two miles southeast of the U.S. 70 and Loop 445 junction, and the city's sewer ordinance names a land application site as the other home for that water. So the plant already holds both of the study's returns, the outfall and the field, and its permit carries a headroom of 1.5 million gallons a day above what the town produces.

Below the outfall, the draw runs east to join Callahan Draw at the head of the White River nine miles west of Floydada, and the White River runs 62 miles through Blanco Canyon to the Salt Fork in Kent County (survey names). The canyon's own record says the river is mostly dry now because of pumping, which is what makes a steady municipal return upstream of it worth reading as a headwater. White River Reservoir, impounded in 1963 sixteen miles southeast of Crosbyton, holds the drinking water of Ralls, Spur, Post, and Crosbyton, and has been through severe drought in recent years (state reservoir page; Post's utility page). Every parameter the Plainview plant returns is a parameter those four towns drink, diluted by whatever the canyon adds.

Three things a retrofit would change, in the order a first lap can afford them:

1. **The receipt.** The plant's daily readings -- flow, oxygen demand, solids, ammonia, phosphorus, chloride, and whichever the permit names -- signed by the operator's key at the moment they are taken, appended to a log that only grows, folded on anyone's phone. The tree's five primitives carry a door reading unchanged, and the `works.doors` tool the Gleaner note proposed offers the fold out. This costs a services agreement and a small terminal at the plant.
2. **The field.** Reuse-grade water to a fibre trial on leased acres beside the plant. Texas reclaimed-water rules (30 TAC Chapter 210, Type II) cover irrigation of a non-food crop at oxygen-demand and fecal limits any modern plant clears, as the readings note found for Lubbock's system. The Plainview hemp variety trial the Brazos study cited already runs under irrigation. A lease turns *disposal by the acre-foot* into *a product by the acre-foot*, which is the whole Civic difference.
3. **The retrofit.** Nutrient recovery ahead of the outfall and a named home for the sludge, so the residual is a receipt before the plant changes shape. The pumped-loop clause from *where the water enters* governs: the residual is named before the plant.

## 7. The first lap -- Simple, Lovable, Complete

**Simple.** One plant, one page, one signature. A services agreement with the City of Plainview for the receipt at the reclamation plant, folded publicly, with the gauge board at the park as its visible face. Scope: the daily reading and nothing else.

**Lovable.** The gauge in the park, and the field. A lease of reclaimed-water acres beside the plant for the fibre trial, its acre-feet declared before the first seed, its bale carrying the water it grew on. Both are things a person in Plainview would want on the day they arrive.

**Complete.** The loop closes when the reading folds on a phone in Post, whose reservoir the return feeds, and matches the gauge in Plainview's park. Then the retrofit lap opens on its own merits, with the residual named first.

The Gallatin ranking holds here word for word: the receipt first, because it is the cheapest and highest-trust move on the river; the lease second, because it turns disposal into product with a permit that already exists; the retrofit third, because it wants a design and a residual home; and a new plant nowhere, because the plant the town needs is standing beside the draw with 1.5 million gallons a day of permitted headroom.

## 8. Why the seats sit in this order

**Post is second, and it is the seat the heart prefers.** A council-manager county seat founded as an experiment in making a town from cereal money, with a textile mill that ran from 1913 into the 1980s, a restored main street, two theatres, an art center in the old hotel, a museum in West Texas's first sanitarium, and a trade day on the mill grounds. It sits on the caprock between the two forks, ten miles from the North Fork's perennial reach, and it drinks the reservoir Plainview's return feeds. Two readings hold it in second place. Its population fell from 4,790 in 2020 to an estimated 3,486 in 2023, and the county from 5,816 to an estimated 4,510 in 2025, so a decade of housing and civic investment is a bet against a trend. And its water is three-sourced under drought, so the first works Post needs is the reservoir's, which stands in Crosby County under a four-town district. Post is the second lap, and the first town that reads Plainview's gauge.

**Graham is third.** Home-rule seat above the first big lake, with a square that already works as a stage, a state music designation, a 6 MGD plant, and two lakes that carry the whole supply. Its open question is supply through a dry decade rather than quality, and the honest first move there is the twin gauge above and below the lakes, the West Yellowstone move from the Gallatin study.

**Granbury and Glen Rose hold the commons and the wrong end of the river.** Granbury runs membranes because the Salt Fork's springs salt its lake, and the Civic reading of a works there is plain: treating at the intake rewards gallons treated, while the good is salt kept out three hundred river miles upstream. Glen Rose has the state's largest permanent amphitheater and a county district with a young reservoir, and a syndicate that wants a stage first would start there. Both are lovable; neither is upstream.

**Clovis is the campus, and the window is open.** The readings note found the authority's plant unfunded, the residual undecided, the isolation reading assemblable on the authority's own numbers, the reuse permit undemanding, the cheese plant as the nutrient anchor, and the hemp haul beyond the economic radius. The syndicate's touch there stays beside a public plant, and the campus is four doors and two states' law at once, which is beyond Simple for a first lap.

**Marlin is the hardest case, and a later lap.** Its plant has state violations for residual and turbidity, loan money repurposed in December 2025 to swap membranes for gravity filters, and a winter of halted production, main breaks, and church kitchens serving hundreds of meals a day. A syndicate's honest door there is contract operations and the receipt, and the reward measure is days without a boil notice. It fails the first axis and passes the "hardest solvable" test better than any town on the river; the receipt tool proven at Plainview is the thing to carry there.

## 9. Projections

**A receipt services agreement is signed within two quarters of a first meeting.** Horizon: six months from the first council briefing. Assumptions: the plant already keeps daily lab readings for its permit; the council reads the gauge board as a civic asset. Falsifier: the utility director declines to publish daily readings, or the council routes a small services contract through a bond-style process. Confidence: plausible.

**The reuse lease for a fibre trial is authorized within one growing season.** Horizon: the 2027 planting. Assumptions: the city's existing land application authorization or a Chapter 210 Type II authorization covers the leased acres; the variety trial's cultivar reads under the total-THC line. Falsifier: the state requires a new authorization whose clock runs past the planting window. Confidence: plausible.

**A Chapter 2267 comprehensive agreement for the reuse retrofit is reached within three years.** Horizon: 2029. Assumptions: the city adopts partnership guidelines; the proposal beats the city's own loan-and-design-build path on delivery or cost. Falsifier: the city takes the state loan and a conventional design-build with no private partner, which is the base-rate path. Confidence: a live possibility.

**Post's population keeps falling through the decade.** Horizon: the 2030 census. Assumptions: the oilfield and ranch economy stays as it is; the reservoir's supply stays strained. Falsifier: a 2030 count above 4,000. Confidence: likely. This is the reading that keeps Post in second place.

## 10. Base rates, before the story

Most Texas water and wastewater plants are financed by their public owners through the state water board's loans and their own bonds; the board's FY2026 wastewater priority list is populated by cities and districts, and private capital enters most often as design-build or contract operations. Counsel holds no count of partnership-act comprehensive agreements naming a water facility since 2011, and that count is the falsifier for the third projection. Reclaimed-water irrigation on the plains is ordinary rather than novel: Lubbock holds a right to land-apply up to 18,430 acre-feet a year, and Plainview's own ordinance names its site. Small county seats on the plains lose people at the rates Garza County shows, while Plainview has held near 20,000 from the 2020 census to its 2026 self-description. And most first hemp fields on new ground miss their planning yield in year one, as the Brazos study read from the Lubbock trials.

## 11. Structural and cyclical, sorted

Structural: the outfall as the White River's headwater; the county seat and its charter; the university; the interstate's federal expansion designation; the Canadian River supply with the Logan works behind it; the salt springs below the forks; the four-town reservoir's dependence on what comes down the canyon. Cyclical: the drought stage; the reinvestment zone's window; the federal hemp definition's delay; the state's supplemental-appropriation deadline of 2027-08-31; the ownership of the closed meatpacking site; the plant's permit renewal cycle.
