## The two words, one at a time

**Residue** is the plant material a harvest leaves behind. A cotton picker takes the white
boll and leaves the stalk standing in the field. A sorghum combine takes the grain and
leaves the stalk. A wheat harvest leaves straw. A cotton gin cleans the fiber and piles up
"gin trash" -- stems, leaves, and short fiber -- by the thousand tons. All of that is
residue. It grew already. Somebody already paid for the water, the seed, and the season
that made it. Then the part people wanted got taken, and this part stayed.

**Leads** means: goes first in the plan. The study ranks the materials a fiber project on
this plain could use, and residue sits at the top of that ranking. First choice, first
lap. The other candidates stay on the list -- they just come later, and each has to earn
its place.

So "residue leads" means: **start with the stalks and straw already lying in the fields,
before planting anything new.**

## Why residue goes first on this plain

The study's numbers make the case in three short steps.

**The rain is spoken for.** This plain gets 350 to 550 mm of rain a year, and the sun and
wind could evaporate 1,500 to 1,750 mm. The gap is filled by pumping the Ogallala, an
aquifer that refills slower than it is pumped. A new crop planted just for fiber would ask
for water this ground has already promised elsewhere. Residue asks for none. Its water bill
was paid by the crop it came from.

**The haul eats the value.** Fresh hemp stalk would ride a truck about 309 miles to the
nearest fiber gin, at roughly 50 to 60 dollars a ton. Against stalk prices of 60 to 125
dollars a ton, the truck eats a third of the value on a good day and all of it on a bad
one. Cotton and sorghum residue is already in the county, beside gins and barns that
already handle it. Short hauls keep the value at home.

**The license comes before the yield.** Hemp is legal by a chemistry test, and the seed
lines that grew best in the Lubbock trials failed that test. Picking a lawful line that
also yields well is its own project. Residue has no such question. A cotton stalk needs no
license.

## What residue becomes

The foundation's own table names the products for this climate row: **insulating infill**
(chopped stalk packed into walls to hold warmth) and **panel and reinforcement** (fiber
pressed into boards). These are building materials, made close to the fields, for the
towns beside them. That is the whole idea of the corridor: grow, process, and build within
a short drive, and keep a receipt at every step.

## What comes after residue

The ranking has a door for each later candidate, and each door has a test.

- **Hemp** enters only on reclaimed water -- the city's cleaned wastewater -- never on
  aquifer water, and only with a lawful seed line. The study even names its own exit: two
  seasons of hemp under a yield floor, and the plan promotes residue back to the whole job.
- **Flax** stays off this ground. June heat and hard winter freezes close both of its
  planting windows.
- **Dryland organic cotton** is named as a teacher, not a crop for this corridor. The
  co-op families around Lubbock grow it on rain and track every bale by the farmer's name,
  and have since 1993. The corridor borrows their receipt-keeping, and keeps its own fiber
  list to hemp and linen.

## The one-sentence version

The fields here already grow tons of fiber every year as a side effect; use that first,
because its water is already paid, its haul is short, and its paperwork is simple -- and
make anything new prove itself before it earns an acre.
