## Why the Tradition Already Solved This

A person is not one thing. The classical Jyotisha tradition knew this and built for it: it divided a single birth chart into many **divisional charts** -- the *vargas* -- each a finer lens on one facet of a life. The whole (the *rashi*, D1) is the body; each division past it reads a different question with its own map. A network that wants members to carry *roles* -- a fighting style here, a way of solving there, a deeper calling beneath -- does not need to invent a role system. Three of the classical divisions already name exactly the three roles a healthy constellation wants, and one older idea, the **three modes**, already names how a group of three divides its labor.

This paper reads four classical ideas for structure only -- the three modes, and the D3, D5, and D9 divisions -- and hands the buildable shape to the silo. Where the tradition's orthodoxy is thin (the fifth division is less canonical than the ninth), the paper says so and marks the mapping as *our adaptation*, not the tradition's claim.

## Room One -- The Three Modes: How a Triad Divides Its Work

Before the divisions, the modes. Classical astrology sorts the twelve signs into three **modes** (the *chara*, *sthira*, and *dvisvabhava* qualities -- cardinal, fixed, and dual), and those three are the natural functions of any group that must both start things and keep them:

| Mode (ASCII Sanskrit) | Western name | The function it names |
|---|---|---|
| **chara** | cardinal / movable | the **initiator** -- opens, proposes, sets a thing moving |
| **sthira** | fixed | the **sustainer** -- holds, stabilizes, sees a thing through |
| **dvisvabhava** | dual / mutable | the **adapter** -- bridges, translates, connects to the next group |

A triad typed by mode is a whole small organism: one to begin, one to hold, one to connect. This is why the base group of three is not only *odd enough to vote clear* but *complete enough to act* -- it already contains the initiator, the sustainer, and the bridge. And the typing **recurses**: three triads make a nine, and the three triads themselves can be a cardinal triad, a fixed triad, and a dual triad -- the same three functions, one scale up. As above, so below, in the plainest possible sense.

## Room Two -- The Three Divisional Roles

Past the modes, three divisions give a member three orthogonal roles -- three questions a person answers differently, three lenses that together sketch a character.

**D3 -- the drekkana -- fighting technique.** The third division classically reads *parakrama*: valor, prowess, the courage of the arms, and the siblings who stand beside you in a contest. Read for a network's roles, the drekkana is **how a member contends** -- their fighting technique, the style they bring to a challenge or a competition. One member fights by speed, another by patience, another by misdirection; the drekkana names which.

**D5 -- the panchamsa -- problem solving.** The fifth division is the least canonical of the three in the classical sixteen, yet the tradition associates the fifth place and its harmonics with *buddhi* -- discerning intelligence, creative power, the merit that lets a mind find a way through. Read for roles -- and here the paper is honest that this is *our* adaptation more than the tradition's fixed claim -- the panchamsa is **how a member solves**: the shape of their problem-solving, the circle of thought they reach for when a thing is stuck. It is the same "circle a thing solves its problems with" the season already gave the star tier.

**D9 -- the navamsa -- dharma, the role essence.** The ninth division is the tradition's deepest and most trusted lens -- the *navamsa*, read for *dharma*, the inner strength, the fruit and essence of the whole chart, the truth a life ripens toward. Read for roles, the navamsa is a member's **class**: their dharma-role, the responsibility they are actually for, in the sense a well-made game gives a character a class that is more than a costume. Where the drekkana is how you fight and the panchamsa is how you think, the navamsa is *who you are for* -- the station you would keep even after the fight and the puzzle are over.

Three roles, then, orthogonal and worn together: a fighting technique, a way of solving, and a dharma-class. A member is a point in that three-space, and two members who share a navamsa share a calling even when their techniques and their problem-solving differ entirely.

## Room Three -- Roles Worn, and Shared Across Skies

A role a member carries is a role others can find them by. This is where the divisional roles meet the social layer the prior paper named: a member "wears" a role the way one wears an outfit for an occasion, and wearing it connects them to everyone standing in that same role in another constellation entirely. All the navamsa-kin across the network -- everyone of one dharma-class -- form a guild that crosses every local tree; all who share a drekkana fighting style can spar and teach across constellations; the modes connect the initiators to the initiators. The graph is no longer only "who sponsors whom" but "who shares my calling, my technique, my way of thinking, somewhere else."

If the outfit is to be more than a figure of speech, let its cloth be honest: a role worn in this network is cut from **hemp, linen, or ramie** -- the low-water, quick-drying, sustainably farmed bast fibers -- never cotton's thirst nor a synthetic's fossil thread. The fabric a thing is imagined in is a quiet statement of what it values, and this network values what grows without exhausting the ground.
