## The finding, first

**The farthest-upstream source of the Missouri already has what it needs, and the farthest-upstream *load* is a hundred and fifty miles away in three different directions.** Brower's Spring sits at roughly 8,800 feet in the Centennial Mountains of Beaverhead County, on the north fork of Hell Roaring Creek, inside country that a national wildlife refuge and a continental-divide ridgeline already keep quiet. A treatment works there would have nothing to treat. What that ground earns is a gauge and its own long public record.

The rungs that reward a works are the first towns on each fork: **West Yellowstone** on the Madison, **Big Sky** on the Gallatin, and **Bozeman** on the East Gallatin. Of these, Bozeman's plant is the largest single point source in the basin and supplies about a third of the East Gallatin's summer flow, and Big Sky already runs the model the whole basin is heading toward -- treat well, discharge to no river at all, and put the water back to work on land.

And the basin is closed. The Upper Missouri accepts no new water-right permits, which turns every water question in the valley from *where do we find more* into *how do we use what we have twice*. That single fact is why a fibre corridor belongs in this study: **hemp and flax retted on the field draw no water and hold no water right.** In the Gallatin Valley that is the strongest agricultural argument on the table, and it is hydrology rather than fashion.

The binding constraint on the fibre half is the same one the country carries -- straw under contract, then spinning -- with one Montana difference worth the whole trip: a five-ton-an-hour decortication line already runs at Fort Benton, and an all-American hemp shirt already exists because of it.

The identification is old and settled. A surveyor named Jacob V. Brower fixed this spring as the utmost source in 1888, visited it in 1895, and published the finding in 1896; he had earlier fixed the Mississippi's source at Lake Itasca. His reading sits about 100 miles east of the point Meriwether Lewis reported in 1805 above Lemhi Pass. From the spring the water runs down Hell Roaring Creek into the Red Rock River, through the Upper and Lower Red Rock Lakes, into Lima Reservoir and then Clark Canyon Reservoir, out as the Beaverhead, joins the Big Hole to become the Jefferson, and meets the Madison and the Gallatin at Missouri River Headwaters State Park at Three Forks, where the Missouri takes its name.

**Reading the ladder honestly: the highest-value work at this rung is a gauge and a boundary.** The boundary largely exists. The gauge is the contribution available to anyone who wants it, and a continuous, signed, publicly foldable record from the utmost source of North America's longest river system is a civic artifact with no substitute -- the baseline every argument below it would otherwise have to invent.

The Missouri at Three Forks is the sum of the Jefferson, the Madison, and the Gallatin. Each fork carries its own first town.

**The one number that decides the ranking.** The Bozeman plant's effluent has accounted for about a third of the East Gallatin's total summer flow -- a stream that runs low in summer over ground that already carries elevated background nutrients. That combination is why the Montana Department of Environmental Quality wrote unusually tight limits for this plant, and why the operator's own margin against those limits is the most consequential water fact in the valley.

**The rung that reads most instructive is Big Sky**, because it holds no permit to discharge to the Gallatin at all. Its treated water waits in ponds through the winter and irrigates the Big Sky, Yellowstone Club, and Spanish Peaks golf courses through the summer. That is lap three of the works shape -- the second use -- reached ahead of the rest of the basin. The honest companion fact: a Bozeman environmental law centre has litigated over pond seepage, and state monitoring has continued to find nutrients reaching the Gallatin, which is exactly the `storage_integrity` field in the reuse shape earning its place.

**The finding a Civic reader must not skip.** The Gallatin is genuinely listed as impaired for algal growth, and after several years of the study that listing triggered, the state has said its data so far shows no indication that excess nutrients are causing the blooms. The Gallatin River Task Force reads the same puzzle from the other side: this river carries very low nutrient levels and still blooms, above and below Big Sky alike. Light, temperature, flow, groundwater exchange, and natural phosphorus in the local geology are all in the frame.

That is the condition the foundation warns about, standing in daylight. **A programme that rewards nutrient reduction, in a river whose blooms may be driven by something else, would spend a decade of public money on a number that was never the constraint.** The Civic move is to keep funding the gauge as load-bearing, size the works to the diagnosis when the load study lands, and write the outcome -- the stream's own reading on aquatic life and recreation -- into every contract rather than the tonnage removed.

That is a mill this corridor can *contract with* long before it considers building one.

## 5. Where the works belongs

The load ladder gives the answer in one line: **the first load on each fork, sized to a diagnosis, aimed at reuse.** Three sites, three different jobs.

**West Yellowstone, the Madison fork.** The highest genuine municipal load in the basin, a gateway town whose summer peak overwhelms a plant designed for a quieter era, already building its answer. The useful contribution here is neither capital nor concrete: it is the **gauge and the ledger** -- above and below the new plant, published continuously, so a town that just spent thirty-eight million dollars can prove what it bought. A works this new deserves a record that outlives its warranty.

**Big Sky, the Gallatin fork.** The reuse model already exists and its open question is named and litigated: whether the storage holds what it says. The contribution is the **storage-integrity receipt** -- instrumented seepage monitoring around the holding ponds, signed by an independent hydrologist, folded publicly. This is the smallest, cheapest, highest-trust intervention available anywhere in the basin, and it converts a lawsuit into a measurement.

**Bozeman, the East Gallatin fork.** The largest load, the tightest permit, the fastest-growing town, and a plant with real headroom between its 8.5 MGD capacity and the 13-plus MGD its site was laid out for. Two horizons live here. The near one is **nutrient recovery**: a plant already stripping nitrogen and phosphorus biologically is a plant whose phosphorus could be crystallised and sold rather than landfilled, which turns a disposal cost into a product with a buyer. The far one is **the second use** -- treated water to irrigation, recharge, or a crop, which in a closed basin is the only new water the valley can create.

**And the honest ranking of the three, by change bought per dollar spent:** the storage receipt at Big Sky first, the twin gauges at West Yellowstone second, nutrient recovery at Bozeman third, and a new plant nowhere, because the plants that matter are built or building.

falsifier              measured seepage above the permitted rate
```

**The collision.** The resource-recovery sector the maintainer named -- struvite from wastewater, biochar from sludge, mineral ash into concrete -- meets this tree's own agricultural ethic head-on, and the meeting deserves a plain sentence rather than a quiet dodge.

*Atthowe ecological* raises fertility from plants: compost, green manures, and the rotation itself, with animal inputs left out because concentrated manure and slurry are the largest single source of agricultural nutrient runoff. **Municipal biosolids are a third category**, neither plant-raised nor animal, and they carry a live contamination question of their own in the persistent fluorinated compounds that treatment plants concentrate. Struvite -- a magnesium-ammonium-phosphate crystal precipitated from the water rather than harvested from the solids -- is a different material from sludge and a different question.

**Counsel's honest position: this is a certifier's ruling rather than a writer's.** The corridor's rule is that a fibre field growing under the tree's ethic states its inputs in the ledger, and the biocyclic and organic certifiers say what qualifies. Naming the tension in the open is worth more than a confident answer, and the ledger makes the answer checkable either way.

                       adds a light irrigation pass that the basin closure makes expensive
```

The Gallatin's retting question inverts the Missouri River valley's. Kansas City's risk is a wet fortnight over-retting a windrow; Bozeman's is a dry September leaving the pectin intact. The response is the same instrument: the bounded timer already shipping in this tree, with `start` and `status`, READY rising when the days complete, and every refusal named -- `DaysOutOfBound`, `NameTooLong`, `LogFull`, `HomeUnavailable`. Add a moisture reading at the bale and a soil reading at the field edge and the batch log becomes a water receipt in a basin where receipts are the currency.

And the reason field retting settles the matter here: **untreated water-retting liquor has measured an average biological oxygen demand near 1,460 mg/L** (semi-industrial flax basins, published 2018), several times untreated household sewage. In a closed basin above a listed river, that process is unthinkable and the field is the vat.

**The mill answer for this valley is a contract rather than a building.** Three readings point the same way.

The nearest fibre line runs at Fort Benton, about four hours north, purpose-built for exactly this crop and already handling flax. Its capacity dwarfs anything a first Gallatin lap would need.

Gallatin Valley industrial space is expensive. Bozeman commercial asking rents run roughly USD 18 to 28 per square foot per year across property types, with industrial available space measured in the low hundreds of thousands of square feet (a commercial listings aggregator, 2026); an earlier local brokerage read warehouse space near USD 1.07 per square foot per month. Against Kansas City's metro asking rate of USD 5.43 per square foot per year, **the same mill building costs something like two to three times more here**, and a named gap remains until a Belgrade or Three Forks broker quotes light industrial with bale-yard land.

And the acreage is thin. Montana harvested about 2,400 acres of hemp state-wide in 2024, most of it in the Golden Triangle north of here rather than in Gallatin County.

So the first lap is: **grow the straw, ret it on the field, bale it, and truck it to the line that already exists** -- with a Gallatin bale yard and a grading floor as the only local capital. A mill in this valley earns its own building on the day the acres justify one, and the falsifier is plain: fewer than 400 contracted acres inside two seasons means the corridor is a supply arrangement rather than a mill.

**What the trials say about yield.** MSU's eastern research centre, under irrigation on silty clay loam, read biomass at 2,359 lb/acre for X-59, 2,285 for CFX-1, and 1,892 for Altair in one season's fibre-focused trial. Those are Sidney numbers on the state's other side, and the Gallatin's shorter, cooler, drier season will read differently. The Fort Benton processor's own 2023 varietal work, run across both dryland and irrigated Montana ground, found X-59 vigorous and excellent at suppressing weeds with moderate yield -- which is the profile a plant-raised fertility programme wants, because weed suppression is the thing a veganic field buys with canopy rather than with chemistry.

## 9. Hemp or linen?

**Hemp leads and flax has a live case here, which is a different answer than Kansas City's.**

Hemp leads on infrastructure: a licensing programme, a processor, a varietal record, and a proven route to a finished garment all exist inside the state. Montana runs its own USDA-approved hemp plan, effective 27 January 2023, administered by the Department of Agriculture under MCA 80-18-101 through 111; licensing runs in two stages, a Planting Permit and then a Production License, with the 2026 application period open.

Flax earns its trial on climate rather than on infrastructure. Fibre flax wants a cool season and very little water, matures in about a hundred days, suppresses weeds through a high seeding rate, and leaves a clean field behind. The Oregon breeding programme that has been selecting fibre flax since 2016 found that autumn sowing let them grow it with no irrigation at all -- and it sourced seed from an organic farm near Bozeman. Montana Fibershed's own multi-year flax study, reporting in 2026, found earlier plantings gave hardier and more consistent growth, and noted the 2026 spring arrived with below-average snowpack and swinging temperatures.

The Fort Benton line runs flax on the same equipment as hemp. **That single fact makes a Gallatin flax trial cheap to try and cheap to finish**, and it is the strongest argument for keeping both fibres in the corridor's name.

The honest limit on flax: harvesting fibre flax wants a puller rather than a combine, and pullers were until recently absent from the United States. That is a shared-equipment question for the trial's second year and a named gap for its first.

## 11. The brand, read by role

The Kansas City study read four houses by role: made-at-origin, chemical-free direct, domestic basics, and the one-collection atelier. This basin adds a fifth reading, and it is the most instructive of the set because it actually happened here.

**The proof-of-concept house.** A Great Falls apparel company, an offshoot of a regional farm-and-outdoors retail chain, worked with the Fort Benton processor to make a work shirt that stayed inside the country end to end. The hemp came off a family farm north of Fort Benton in 2020. The fibre travelled to North Carolina to be refined and blended with Arizona cotton, spun in North Carolina, woven in South Carolina, finished in Georgia, and cut and sewn in New York City. The first run was 239 shirts. It reached the market in December 2025 at USD 150, and a roll of six hundred yards of the finished fabric disappeared somewhere in transit and has stayed lost.

Read the Civic way, that shirt teaches four things at once. **The fibre is ready; the chain is the work.** The processor's own reading is that the hard part was getting decorticated fibre to a quality that a spinner could actually use, and that the freight -- the fibre moving many more times than it needed to -- was the real enemy. The retailer's reading is that the price tag needs a story beside it or it reads as an ordinary button-up.

**The house this corridor would build** takes provenance from the made-at-origin house, process purity from the chemical-free house, patience and daily wearability from the domestic basics house, permanence from the atelier, and the proof-of-concept house's hard-won map of who can actually spin and weave in this country. And it adds the thing none of the five can show yet: **a receipt from the field, through the retting timer, the bale, the grading floor, the spinner, and the sewing floor, that anyone can fold without trusting the brand.**

On this ground the receipt has an extra line no other corridor can write: *this crop drew no water in a closed basin, and here is the log*.

## 12. Projections

**The storage-integrity receipt at Big Sky is buildable inside one season.** Horizon: twelve to eighteen months from a first meeting. Assumptions: the district accepts instrumented monitoring; an independent hydrologist signs; the readings publish whole. Falsifier: a refusal to publish, which would say the appetite for verification is lower than the appetite for the argument. Confidence: plausible.

**The Gallatin's load study will name something other than nutrients, or name nutrients with a qualifier.** Horizon: through the study's completion. Assumptions: the pattern in the published data holds -- low nutrient concentrations alongside persistent blooms above and below the resort. Falsifier: a TMDL that names a controllable nutrient load with a clear dose-response. Confidence: a live possibility rather than a forecast, and the reason the gauge outranks the works.

**A Gallatin fibre trial can run on contract inside two seasons.** Horizon: two growing seasons. Assumptions: 200 to 400 acres of hemp under a straw contract to the existing line, a 2 to 5 acre flax strip beside it, field retting logged in the timer. Falsifier: fewer than 400 contracted acres by the second spring, which makes this a supply arrangement rather than a corridor. Confidence: plausible.

**Domestic spinning stays the binding link.** Horizon: five to eight years. Assumptions: the existing scattered chain -- refine and blend in one state, spin in another, weave in a third, finish in a fourth -- remains the only route. Falsifier: a domestic hemp spinning line commissioned within three years. Confidence: likely.

## 13. Base rates, before the story

Most first mills in a young fibre industry run below nameplate for their first two seasons, and the ones that close usually close for want of straw or buyers rather than for want of a machine. Montana's 2,400 harvested acres in 2024 put it well behind the leading states, so a Gallatin corridor starts from a small base in a state that is itself a small base. A municipal treatment plant takes years from facility plan to commissioning and lasts twenty to thirty years, which means the plants now going in at West Yellowstone and Big Sky have already set this basin's shape until roughly mid-century. And a six-year water-quality study is an ordinary length for a contested impairment rather than a delay.

None of that argues for waiting. All of it argues for putting money into gauges and contracts, which are fast and cheap, before putting money into concrete, which is slow and permanent.

## 14. Structural and cyclical, sorted

**Structural:** the elevation and the ladder; the basin closure and the water-rights arithmetic behind it; the cold, low-nutrient character of headwater streams; the existing decortication capacity at Fort Benton; the state's own hemp plan; a university in the valley; and the fact that a field-retted fibre crop asks the water office for nothing.

**Cyclical:** the Gallatin Valley's industrial rents and its recent building wave; the resort-tax revenue that funded the Big Sky plant; the federal nutrient-recovery and low-carbon materials programmes, whose standing in 2026 wants checking before a plan leans on them; and hemp acreage nationally, which moves with the cannabinoid market that shares its drying and processing capacity.

## What this refuses, and the named gaps

**It seats nothing.** A programme for Montana wants the professionals ahead of the prose, and the district engineers, the hydrologists, and the water attorneys come first. **It sizes no plant and names no cause for the Gallatin's blooms.** **It carries no household numbers**; the fund, the sleeves, and the family's allocation live in the maintainer's field.

**Seven gaps are named rather than filled:** a Belgrade or Three Forks light-industrial rent quote with bale-yard land; the Bozeman plant's current average daily flow against its 8.5 MGD capacity; Montana's specific reclaimed-water rule text; whether a second use can offset a consumptive claim under the closure; a straw contract and a grade specification from the Fort Benton line; a flax puller within reach; and a certifier's ruling on whether recovered struvite qualifies under a plant-raised fertility standard. The first five turn this study into a plan; the last two decide what the corridor may honestly claim.
