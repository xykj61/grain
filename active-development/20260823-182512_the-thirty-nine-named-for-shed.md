## So the shed answer is a plain one

**All thirty-nine stay, and the reason is arithmetic rather than sentiment.** Three conditions have
to hold before a cut, and this set meets none of them:

1. **A living mutant seats first.** [`molt.md`](../.claude/rules/molt.md) is explicit -- a fossil joins
   Class H once an accrete copy at a fresh stamp has taken over as the living path. All thirty-nine
   remain the living record of their own subject, each still awaiting any mutant at all.
2. **Class O wants unreachability**, defined as *no file in the tree names it*. The floor for this set
   is **three** citing files and the ceiling is forty-six. Class O membership is settled by the shred
   census rather than by a browse, and the census seats none of these.
3. **The cut itself stays RED until Keaton circles it by name.** A molt pass readies; a circled
   **shred** cuts. This pass opened nothing.

A fourth reason stands on its own, and it is the one that would bite hardest. **These are dated
testimony.** Their basenames carry a one-clock stamp, so the mark law places them
in the tier that is *resolved rather than rewritten* -- and the same-day crawl from the root README
found **1,097 of the tree's 1,209 broken links already living in that tier**, held readable by
exactly this rule. Cutting thirty-nine cited documents would raise that number rather than lower it.

## The falsifier

This pass claims the thirty-nine are load-bearing. The check is cheap and belongs to whoever doubts
it: pick the lowest-cited of them, `20260727-101510_the-acme-onboarding-walk.md` at three citing
files, read those three, and see whether each still needs it. Should all three turn out to
hold it by habit, and the next two above it as well, then the citation count measures habit rather
than need, and the shed conditions want a stricter reading than *is it named anywhere*.
