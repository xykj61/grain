## The short name

**Lila-first, return-first.**

Lila is the glad keystone. The return is the ground that lasts. The name leads with the play. When you choose, you answer the return first, and then you answer Lila on that ground.

## The order, in plain words

When you choose the next chapter, season, journey, quest, or single round, choose in this order.

1. **The Long Return.** Do the work that will still read true on the far side of the years. A front door, a foundation, a name, a witness, the reference weave. A person meets a front door thousands of times. A one-day patch is met once. Hours given to what lasts keep giving. Hours given to what fades are spent once.
2. **Lila, within that tier.** Among work of equal lasting weight, take the one glad keystone. It is the hardest move you can still finish. When it is done, the rest of the road opens. Safety has already agreed, and performance has already agreed, and joy earns the vote. That rank is Lesson 4. Lila is the play of making the true thing with a light hand, and the offering of leaving it where a person can hold it.

Reds come before either of these. A wrong thing is repaired before new lasting work begins. The Long Return orders the building. Reds order the repair.

Ship one real thing before you name the next. One keystone a round, unless that round's own shape bounds more. When Keaton says **align**, the plan is reordered by this page, and a seated brief with green witnesses wins.

A chapter's four seasons, a season's journeys, a journey's quests: each ring leads with its own long return, and with the Lila inside it. The tree's ladders run in fours.

## The long-return test

Before a front door or a foundation ships, ask:

> Will this still be true, and still read plainly, three years from now, after the code moves and the season turns?

A line about this week's status wears a status mark. A clever word yields to a plain one. A claim binds to a witness. What survives the three belongs in the opening paragraph.

*May the work we choose today still be warm when a stranger meets it. May the hard move be a glad one. And may the tree grow the way a tended garden does, one true season at a time.*
