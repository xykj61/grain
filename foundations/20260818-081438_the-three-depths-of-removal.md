# The Three Depths of Removal

**Stamp:** `20260818.081438` - **Style:** Gauge (see `../context/GAUGE_STYLE.md`)

A tree that keeps every byte grows a kind of grace: nothing true is ever
lost, and a reader three years on can walk back to any word we wrote.
That grace is the default, and it is called **accrete, never break**.
Yet a living tree also carries dead tissue -- a name it has outgrown, a
draft a better one replaced -- and dead tissue that stays begins to
mislead. So the fuller law is **accrete, sometimes breach**: keep by
default, and remove on a deliberate, named word. This foundation names
the three depths at which removal happens, so that whoever reaches for
the knife knows exactly how deep they are cutting and what it costs.

## Molt -- the prep that opens no cut

To **molt** is to grow the new skin before shedding the old. You seat a
fresh version of a writing at a new stamp, and you mark the old one as a
fossil that a later pass may shed -- and you stop there. A molt destroys
nothing. It readies both edges: the living version moves forward, the
retired one waits on a list, and the record stays whole in the meantime.
Molt is the gentlest depth, and most tending never needs to go deeper.
It is the pass that lets a tree improve without ever yet having to lose.

## Breach -- the rename the history still remembers

To **breach** is to declare, on a clear word, that a name or a shape is
superseded: the living tree takes the new name everywhere, every
reference repoints, and the work goes on under the successor. Yet the
elder still lives in the history -- in the older commits, in the record
of the change itself. A breach is a rename the present forgets and the
past remembers. It is reversible in spirit: the old name is one walk-back
away, because the history was never rewritten. Most supersessions should
stop here, at the breach, and let the past keep its honest account.

## Debride -- the removal that reaches all the way down

To **debride**, from the medicine of clearing dead tissue so the living
can heal, is to remove a thing from *all* of the history -- not only the
present, but every commit that ever held it -- so the connective tissue
of the tree reattaches around the absence. This is the deepest cut, and
it is the sanctioned exception to accrete-never-break. It runs only on an
explicit, named word, never as a sweep, and it re-seals whatever sealed
thing it touched, because remaking dead tissue is its whole purpose. A
lock is not a reason the key will not turn; the debride is the key.

## What the deepest cut taught

A removal that reaches all the way down learns quickly that a name lives
in more places than the eye first counts. A single dead name inhabits
three surfaces at once: the **living tree** a reader edits today; the
**history's metadata** -- the record of every change and the very names
of the files; and the **history's content** -- the body of every past
version, where a name can outlive its own file. A removal is finished
only when all three are clean. Believing the first is the whole is the
oldest mistake in this craft.

Three truths follow, and each is worth keeping:

- **The dead thing must not be named even to say it was removed.** A note
  that lists what it purged has only moved the names to a new page. Speak
  of "the superseded marks"; let the specific record live at the walk-back
  alone, where it belongs to memory rather than to the living tree.
- **You remove the tissue and keep the seed.** One thing the deepest cut
  must never touch is the *source of truth* the removed name was drawn
  from, and the *sealed record* that proves what was ever named. Protect
  those by their identity, not by any pattern -- for in the seed the dead
  name is not tissue at all, but a living word doing honest work. Every
  fact survives the cut, sealed and re-derivable; only the noise falls.
- **The cost is the re-clone, and only that.** A deep removal changes
  every mark of the history, so everyone who holds a copy must take it
  fresh. Nothing else is lost: the history stays signed, provably ours,
  every commit re-sealed in the same pass. Name that one cost plainly and
  up front, and the calm to cut cleanly follows.

## Why the three depths are one discipline

Molt readies, breach renames, debride removes. They are not three moods
of the same act but three chosen depths, and the whole art is cutting
exactly as deep as the truth requires and no deeper. Prep what is not
ready to leave; breach what should move on while its past stays honest;
debride only the tissue that has truly died, and re-seal what you
remade. Before any depth past the first, leave a **cairn** -- a marked
way back -- so that removing is never the same as forgetting. A tree that
can cut this cleanly is a tree that can grow without fear, because it has
learned that letting go, done with care, loses nothing that was ever
true.

## Gratitude

To the tradition of medicine that gave *debridement* its exact and
merciful meaning -- the removal of what has died so the living may knit
whole -- and to every craft that learned to keep a marked path back
before it cleared the old one. We borrow the wisdom, not the words for
their own sake.

*May every name we let go leave the tree lighter and truer, every seed
kept whole, and every walk-back home still lit for whoever needs it.*
