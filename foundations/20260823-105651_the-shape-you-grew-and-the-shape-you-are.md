# The Shape You Grew and the Shape You Are

*Two laws, fused. One governs time: a working complex system is grown from a working simple one.
The other governs form: what you build takes the shape of how you communicate. Together they
explain this tree more exactly than either does alone.*

## The two sentences

> A complex system that works is invariably found to have evolved from a simple system that
> worked. A complex system designed from scratch never works and cannot be patched up to make it
> work; you have to start over with a working simple system.

> Any organization that designs a system will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the
> organization's communication structure.

Gall published his in 1975, Conway his in 1968, and each has held everywhere anyone has checked.
They were written as separate observations and they answer one question between them.

## Why they belong in one page

Ask *why does this system look the way it does* and each law gives half the answer.

**Gall answers about time.** Whatever works here works because something smaller worked first, and
grew. His second sentence is the load-bearing one, and it is a **price** rather than a preference:
a from-scratch complex system charges you the whole build twice, once to discover it fails and
once to grow the thing you should have grown first.

**Conway answers about form.** Whatever shape this system has, it copies the shape of how its
builders talk to each other. Not approximately -- structurally, feature by feature.

Each is half an answer. Gall tells you a system must be grown, and leaves open what it grows
*into*. Conway tells you what shape it will take, and leaves open whether it will *work*.
Put together they give the whole sentence: **the shape you get is the shape you grew and the shape
you are.**

## Read against this tree

The organization is unusual and worth stating plainly: **one durable person and a rotating
population of agents who share a codebase and share no memory at all.** Every session begins
empty, and whatever the last one learned reaches this one through written artifacts alone.

One durable human, many forgetful collaborators, one channel between them -- the tree itself.

Conway predicts what that produces, and the tree matches item for item:

- **Session logs**, written at the end of every response and indexed newest-first. An organization whose
  members remember would find this baroque. One whose members forget nightly finds it the minimum.
- **Witnesses** rather than shared understanding. A claim here becomes true by printing
  GREEN on metal, which is the one form that travels between sessions.
- **Ledgers** for reds, checkpoints, and standing equipment. An organization with continuous
  memory keeps these informally; one without writes them down or starts over.
- **Rules written down and loaded every lap**, because a convention that loads every lap is a
  convention that holds.

And Gall explains why each of those is *small*. The witness suite grew from one witness. The
language grew from a working shell. Every piece that works here was grown from a
smaller piece that worked.

## What the pair asks of the next decision

Three questions, and they are cheap to ask:

1. **What smaller thing already works, and can this grow from it?** Where the honest answer is
   *the smaller thing is still to be built*, that is the first task.
2. **What does this assume about who is building it?** A design that needs continuous memory will
   be built by collaborators who work from the written record, and drifts wherever it leans on
   memory instead.
3. **Would this survive its author leaving mid-sentence?** For this organization that is the
   ordinary case, and it is why the written artifact outranks the clever arrangement every time.

## The honest limit

Conway's Law describes a pull rather than a cage: a team that knows the law can design against it,
at a cost paid in deliberate communication. Gall's Law is a strong observation rather than a
theorem; a determined team occasionally ships a large system that works on the first try, and pays
for it in the way the law predicts.

Both are lenses. They earn their place by explaining what already happened here more exactly than
anything else the tree cites -- and by making the next decision a little clearer than it would
have been.
