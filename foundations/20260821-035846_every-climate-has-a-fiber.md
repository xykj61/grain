## The Claim

A building can be grown.

Not entirely, and not everywhere at once -- yet far more of a wall than the last century assumed. The insulation, the sheathing, much of the interior, and a share of the structure can come out of a field that was bare in spring, and go into a wall that holds warmth and carbon for the length of a human life. What is grown can be grown again. What is quarried and fired cannot.

This document names what such a material owes, and how a society could reward it so that the rewarding actually works. It stays place-neutral on purpose: the crop changes with the climate, and everything else stays the same.

## What a Material Owes

The four promises this project already keeps -- do no harm, tell the truth, hold lightly, be kind -- read cleanly as obligations that matter can carry.

**Do no harm** is the water. A material that leaves a river worse is not a good material, whatever it does for the carbon accounting.

**Tell the truth** is the receipt. Every claim a material makes -- where it grew, how it was fed, what the independent test measured -- is a fact someone signed, or it is marketing.

**Hold lightly** is the return. A material that cannot come apart at the end of the building's life has borrowed from a future that never agreed to lend.

**Be kind** is the hand. The people who grow and process it are paid near where they live, and the value stays in the place that made it.

None of these is a slogan. Each is measurable, and the rest of this document is about measuring them.

## Fertility Without Animals

The tree already names this ethic and does not need a new word for it: **Atthowe ecological**, for the regenerative farmer whose practice it honors -- *not vegan, not plant-based, but ecological*, which is a claim about the land rather than about a diet. Its kin in the wider world is the **biocyclic veganic** standard: fertility raised from plant matter, compost, green manures, and the crop rotation itself, without animal inputs.

The reason this belongs in a materials document rather than only a food one is direct: **the animal input is where most of the water pollution comes from.** Concentrated manure and slurry carry the nitrogen and phosphorus that run off into groundwater and downstream, and the algal blooms and dead zones that follow are the visible end of that chain. Take the animal input out of the fertility system and the largest single source of agricultural nutrient runoff leaves with it.

Fibre crops make this easier than food does. Many of them are undemanding: they want less fertility than a grain does, several fix their own nitrogen, and several suppress weeds densely enough to need little else. A fibre crop grown veganic is not a compromise version of a conventional one -- it is often the easier way to grow it.

## The Waters

Hold the water thesis on its own, because it is the strongest universal in this document. Every climate has water, and every region already knows what its water is worth.

Three refusals carry most of it:

**No animal-sourced fertility**, for the runoff reason above.

**No retting into open water.** Bast fibres are separated from their stalks by retting -- letting the pectin break down so the fibre releases. Done in a river or a pond, retting is a serious pollutant, drawing the oxygen out of the water it sits in. Done on the field where the crop grew, by dew and weather, the same process returns its nutrients to the soil that will grow next year's crop. The technique is old and the difference is total.

**No wet chemical finishing that leaves the loop.** Where a process needs water it recirculates it, and what it discharges it discharges clean or not at all.

There is a fourth, and it is why one famous fibre is absent from this document: **the thirstiest crops are refused outright.** A fibre that drains an aquifer or a lake to grow, and dyes a river downstream, fails the first promise no matter how pleasant the cloth. This project's own habit already reflects it -- when we reach for a fibre as a metaphor we reach for hemp, linen, or ramie, and never for cotton or a synthetic. The habit came from the same reasoning as the policy.

## Every Climate, Its Own Crop

Here is the universalization, and it is the plainest section in the document. **The specification travels; the crop stays local.** A region does not import a material strategy. It reads its own rainfall, its own season length, its own soil, and it grows the fibre that already wants to grow there.

| Where | What that place can grow | What it becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperate maritime** -- cool, damp, long soft season | flax; nettle | linen fibre, and the woody core as insulation and board |
| **Temperate continental** -- warm summer, real winter | hemp; straw from grain rotation | hempcrete-type infill, batt insulation, structural board, baled straw wall |
| **Subtropical and monsoon** -- hot, wet season | jute; kenaf; ramie | board, batt, and fibre-reinforced panel |
| **Arid and semi-arid** -- little rain, strong sun | agave and sisal kin; sorghum and other stalk residue | fibre for panel and reinforcement, stalk for insulating infill |
| **Tropical humid** | coir from coconut husk; abaca; fast bamboo | resilient batt, board, and structural cane |
| **Boreal and alpine** -- short season, thin fibre options | little bast; timber and grain straw | timber structure with straw and wood-fibre insulation |
| **Any of them** | the residue already produced by the food crop | the honest first material, since it needs no new field at all |

Two things are worth reading twice in that table.

The first is the last row. **The cheapest material in every climate is the residue already lying in the field** -- the straw, the stalk, the husk left after a food harvest. It competes with no food crop for land, it is often burned or wasted today, and in most regions it is the correct place to start. A region with no obvious fibre crop is not a region with no material.

The second is the boreal row, which is the honest one. **Not every climate can grow a bast fibre**, and a document that claimed otherwise would be the kind of universal that quietly excludes the places that do not fit. The far north's answer is timber and straw, and that is a real answer rather than a consolation.

## Funding What Is Real

A material like this is usually described as too expensive. That framing is where the civic lens earns its place.

Public money, issued by the authority that brings it into being, is held back less by accounting than by **what is actually real** -- the workers standing ready, the materials at hand, the capacity not yet in use. When people are ready and good work waits to be done, that work can be funded and done, and the binding limit shows itself as rising prices once idle capacity is fully at work rather than as a number in a ledger. Mainstream economics names that tension plainly and both views earn their place in honest debate.

Read that way, the question is not *can it be afforded* but **is the capacity real** -- and for grown materials it usually is. The land exists. The people exist. The processing equipment is unglamorous and well understood. What is missing is a buyer willing to be the first, at a scale that lets a regional mill exist at all.

So the civic instruments are three, and each is Civic Style's first principle applied:

**Procurement preference on measured performance.** Name the outcome plainly -- thermal resistance, compressive strength, carbon held per cubic metre, verified by independent testing at delivery -- and let region-grown materials that meet or beat the specification earn preference. Open competition on the specification keeps the pressure on quality while regional origin adds its own preference, so local industry grows on a genuine advantage rather than on a subsidy that hides its own failure.

**Long performance bonds.** A maker whose material underperforms bears the cost of putting it right, so the reward at sale and the liability after sale point at the same thing: buildings that perform as promised for decades. This is the single instrument that makes a new material trustworthy without waiting a generation for reputation.

**Transition support that names the soil.** Farms reaching the measured outcomes earn preference; farms building toward them earn support. And what is measured is the outcome itself -- soil organic matter, water infiltration, the diversity of living things -- with unannounced spot verification funded as load-bearing infrastructure rather than as a line item that thins in lean years. The whole value of a preference rests on its being real.

Beside the public money, the smaller currency that stays close to home does what it does best: each time it changes hands the value stays in the community rather than draining to a distant headquarters. A regional mill, a local grower, and a builder in the same valley are exactly the circle it was made for. And the supply of such a token is honest when it is **a fold rather than a cap** -- issued minus retired, recomputable by anyone, declared by nobody.

## The Receipts

Every instrument above depends on one thing: that the claims are true and that anyone can check them.

This is where the architecture the tree already carries meets the field. The five primitives -- a keypair a person makes and guards, a signed event, an append-only log, a pure fold, a capability -- compose into a **provenance and performance ledger**: a chain from field to wall recording who grew it, how it was fed, what the independent laboratory measured, what bond stands behind it, and how the soil and water read at the contract and at each renewal.

Three properties make it worth building rather than merely promising:

**Signed at the moment made.** The responsible party signs each record when it is created. Records are append-only; corrections reference their originals; nothing is deleted.

**Checkable from outside.** Anyone can verify the record's integrity without trusting the application at all -- the same principle we hold in code. A skeptic, a journalist, or a competitor can run their own analysis and publish their own findings, with no application and no approval gate.

**Anchored where we do not control it.** Identity rests on a public anchor outside our own tree: proof of control of an on-chain, human-readable name that anyone can check without trusting us, checked live at joining and re-checkable on demand rather than assumed from a stale record. A grower's identity, an independent laboratory's identity, and a bond issuer's identity each rest on ground we cannot quietly edit. That is the point -- **accountability that depends on our good behaviour is not accountability.**

And the ledger should carry the soil facts beside the commercial ones. Organic matter, infiltration, living diversity -- the measurements Civic Style already names -- fit in the same append-only log as the invoice. A ledger that records only what was sold, and not how the place is doing, would be a smaller thing than it could be.

## What This Refuses

An honest vision names its own limits, and this one has four.

**It is not a claim that grown materials suit every building.** Some spans want steel and some foundations want concrete. The claim is about the large, boring, high-volume share of a wall -- insulation, sheathing, infill, interior -- where the alternative is usually a fired or foamed product and the substitution is genuinely available.

**It is not certified by writing it down.** Every performance claim here rests on independent testing that costs real money and takes real time, and materials certification is a slow, expensive, unglamorous road. Nothing in this document shortens it.

**It does not price the food-versus-fibre question away.** Land growing fibre is not growing food, which is exactly why the residue row of the table matters most and why a fibre crop earns its place by fitting into a rotation rather than displacing one.

**And it seats no program anywhere.** These are instruments a jurisdiction could adopt, described place-neutrally. No specific policy is proposed for any specific place in this document, and any that ever were would want the professionals before the prose.

## Why It Belongs Here

Because *ahimsa*, followed far enough down a supply chain, becomes materials science.

A wall grown from soil fed with plants, retted on the field where it grew, holding carbon for sixty years while it keeps a house from needing so much cooling, made by people paid near where they live, carrying a receipt anyone can check -- that is *do no harm* expressed as a building assembly rather than as an intention. It is the same discipline this project already keeps in code, meeting matter.

And it is a householder's dharma at the scale of a region: to shelter by supplying. Provisioning is a form of protection. The reward pointing at the good is not a sentiment here but a specification -- name the outcome, name what you measure, keep the two aligned under every condition, and check the alignment where anyone can see it.

*May every wall hold what it promised for longer than the promise. May the water leaving a field be cleaner than the water that entered it. May the value stay in the place that grew it. And may every receipt tell the truth about a real piece of ground.*
