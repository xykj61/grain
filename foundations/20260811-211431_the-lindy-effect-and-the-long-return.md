## The idea, plainly

The **Lindy effect** is a simple, startling observation: for a thing that does not age in the ordinary way — an idea, a text, a tool, a technology — the longer it has already lasted, the longer it can be expected to last still. A book in print for a century is a better bet to see the next century than a book published last spring. Survival is evidence of fitness to survive.

The key word is **non-perishable**. A person is *perishable*: every year lived shortens the years remaining, because a body wears. A proverb is *non-perishable*: every year it stays in use lengthens its expected future, because it has just passed one more test of whether people still find it worth repeating. Grain's tree is non-perishable in exactly this sense — a plain-text field of code, prose, and witnesses that does not wear with age, only with neglect.

## Where it comes from

The name traces to **Lindy's**, a New York delicatessen where comedians reportedly judged that a performer's future career length tracked their past longevity. **Albert Goldman** wrote the notion up in 1964. **Benoît Mandelbrot**, in *The Fractal Geometry of Nature* (1982), gave it a mathematical home and the name *Lindy effect*. **Nassim Nicholas Taleb** carried it into wide use in *Antifragile* (2012), sharpening the non-perishable condition and tying it to how ideas and technologies actually survive.

The mathematics is a **power law**: when lifetimes follow a Pareto distribution, expected remaining life is proportional to age. That is the whole engine — no mysticism, just the shape of a distribution where the old are, on average, the durable. (Faithful public treatments: Wikipedia's *Lindy effect*; John D. Cook's short derivation; Toby Ord's 2023 note on its limits.)

## Why it belongs in Grain's foundations

A README is the most Lindy-exposed artifact a project owns. It is read first, cited most, and — if it is good — outlives every sprint, every refactor, every season. So we write the front door for the **long return**: the reader who arrives not this week but three years on, and finds it still true, still warm, still pointing at what runs.

The Lindy effect turns three of our standing disciplines from preference into strategy:

- **Favorite words over fashion words.** The [`comlink-tendency`](../.claude/rules/comlink-tendency.md) already reaches for the clearest, most fun, safest word at any length — Comlink, Mantra, Caravan, Tally, Pond, Nib, Rye, Rishi, Kyri, baton. Lindy explains *why this compounds*: a plain word a newcomer grasps on their first day is a word that will still read plainly on their ten-thousandth. Clever coinages age; clear ones don't.
- **Witness before narrative.** The [`two-rooms`](../context/TWO_ROOMS.md) discipline — every claim marked *proven* or *proposed* — is a Lindy safeguard. A doc that only ever claims what a green witness shows cannot rot into a lie as the code moves beneath it. Truth that was checked stays checkable; truth that was merely asserted decays.
- **Accrete, never break.** TAME's tiered accretion (`../context/TAME_GUIDANCE.md` §4) and the one-clock law keep dated testimony readable forever. A tree that never rewrites its past is a tree whose age is *earned* — every old stamp is a survived year, and Lindy says those years are the best predictor of the years to come.

## The long-return test for our prose

Before a front-door or foundation piece ships, ask the Lindy question alongside the Radiant ones:

> *Will this still be true, and still read plainly, three years from now — after the code moves, after the season turns, after the reader who wrote it is gone from the thread?*

If a line depends on this week's status, mark it as status (the two rooms). If it depends on a clever word, prefer the plain one (the comlink tendency). If it depends on a claim, bind it to a witness (docs-implementation-sync). What survives all three is what deserves the opening paragraph.
