# New Gauge Style -- writing that measures, and stays worth reading

*Also called **Gauge Style** in short form, and **Gauge Guidance** where it addresses an agent
rather than a writer -- one style, three names, seated together `20260823.064454`.*

| Name | Reach for it when |
|---|---|
| **New Gauge Style** | the full name, for a heading, a first mention, or a status bar |
| **Gauge Style** | ordinary prose, once the reader has met the full name |
| **Gauge Guidance** | the agent-facing, operational form -- recursion-loop prompts, autopilot instructions, and rules an unattended lap reads. Sibling to **TAME Guidance**, which governs code the same way |

The three carry one meaning. **Gauge Guidance** names the same style pointed at a machine that will
write unattended for hours, so it leans on the checkable parts -- the three settings, their
ceilings, and the meter -- while **New Gauge Style** speaks to a person choosing words.


**Language:** EN
**Seated:** `20260823.045448` on Keaton's word
**Status:** Living -- the working style for technical documentation, analysis, forecasting, ecosystem description, and design essays
**Inherits:** [`RADIANT_STYLE.md`](RADIANT_STYLE.md) (warmth and clarity) - [`CIVIC_STYLE.md`](CIVIC_STYLE.md) (name what you reward) - [`TAME_GUIDANCE.md`](TAME_GUIDANCE.md) (bound everything, say why)
**Kin:** [`TWILIGHT_STYLE.md`](TWILIGHT_STYLE.md) (the nocturne register) - [`TWO_ROOMS.md`](TWO_ROOMS.md) (proven or proposed)

**Purpose:** One style for writing that carries measurements and still welcomes a reader. A gauge
reports a reading exactly, and this style keeps that exactness while adding what a gauge has never
had: warmth, plain language, and a clear sense of who the reader is and what they came for.

## The first rule: don't be too smart about it

Before every other rule in this guide, this one.

**Write so the reader understands, rather than so the writer sounds impressive.** Those two aims
feel similar from the inside and point in opposite directions. When they part, the reader wins,
every time.

Being too smart about it looks like this, and each of these is a habit worth catching in yourself:

- Reaching for the specialist word when a common one carries the whole meaning.
- Compressing a paragraph into an aphorism, because the compression feels elegant to write and
  lands as a riddle to read.
- Naming an idea after something clever rather than after what it does.
- Leaving out the obvious step, on the grounds that a good reader would supply it.
- Stacking three true asides into one sentence because each of them is true.
- Explaining a thing in terms of another thing the reader also does not have yet.

The affirmative form, and the one to hold in mind while drafting:

> **Write it the way you would say it to a friend who is smart, curious, and new here.**

That friend deserves your best thinking and your plainest words at the same time. A page they
follow easily and remember afterward is worth more than a page that impresses them and leaves
nothing behind. Clarity is the harder craft, and it is the one that lasts.

Radiant Style says the same thing in its own register -- *warm and grounded, it feels like a
person rather than a policy document* -- and this is its working form: **the reader's
understanding is the measure, and no other measure outranks it.** Precision serves that. So does
brevity. So does every rule below.

## Why this guide exists

For months this tree labelled its prose **Radiant** and often wrote something else. On `20260823`
Keaton read a freshly written front door and said it was the opposite of the intention.
Measurement agreed, and the numbers matter because the feeling arrived first:

| Document | Sentences carrying a negative | Reading grade |
|---|---|---|
| `README.md`, written that day and called Radiant | **46%** | 8.3 |
| a founding statement, same day, same claim | **54%** | 8.8 |
| `RADIANT_STYLE.md` itself | 29% | 8.0 |

A founding statement twice as negative as the guide it claimed to follow. The reading grade sat
inside target the whole time, which is why nobody caught it: the meter that existed counted
**negation words** across `.claude/rules/*.md` alone, so the front door went unmeasured and
negative *framing* went uncounted.

Both documents were rewritten the same day and now measure **15%** and **11%**. The lesson holds
past the repair: a style label nobody measures is a wish, and the register that had grown here
deserved its own honest name and its own numbers.

## What a gauge is, and why the name fits

A gauge reports a reading. It is precise, it earns trust, and it tells you plainly when a value
sits outside its range. Those are exactly the virtues this tree's writing already had.

A gauge also, left alone, welcomes nobody. So this style keeps the instrument and adds the host:
the same exactness, delivered by someone glad you came.

**A gauge belongs everywhere a number does. It reads best when a person is holding it.**

## What Gauge inherits

### From Radiant Style -- the warmth

Every Radiant move holds here without exception.

- **Lead with what is.** State the capability, the reading, the finding. A reader absorbs the
  first words of a sentence most strongly, so put the substance there.
- **Compare by what keeps, never by what decays.** When two things are weighed against each other,
  name the one that lasts and let the other follow. *A method keeps longer than an answer*, rather
  than *a named winner rots faster than the guide around it*. Both sentences carry the same
  finding; only the second asks the reader to picture decay before they reach the point. The verbs
  to watch are **rots, decays, withers, rusts, dies, goes stale, falls apart** -- each is honest
  about the world and wrong as a sentence's first move, and each is doubly tempting because it
  sounds like wisdom. Seated `20260823.201533` on Keaton's word, after exactly that sentence
  shipped in a Door-setting guide. `rot`, `rots`, and `stale` are already counted by
  `tools/fixtures/prose_register_scan.sh`; this line names the *construction* the count alone
  cannot see.
- **Active voice by default.** Reserve the passive for when the actor genuinely does not matter.
- **Affirmative contrast.** Prefer *rather than* to a heavy *not*, and *yet* to *but*.
- **8th grade through collegiate.** Conversational and eloquent together. Read it aloud; if it
  flows and feels generous, it is close.
- **Name a coined term's plain function on first use** -- and, added here, do the same for a
  coined *maxim*. An aphorism earned inside this tree is opaque outside it.
- **Vary sentence length**, and let each idea land before the next arrives.
- **Spend the benediction where it is earned**, on foundations and front doors.

### From Civic Style -- the alignment

Civic Style asks one question of every policy, and Gauge asks it of every piece of writing that
touches money, incentives, or outcomes.

- **Name what the thing rewards.** Any system, contract, market, or metric rewards something.
  Say what, exactly, and say whether it matches the outcome anyone actually wants.
- **Name the measurement, and name who verifies it.** A standard is worth the rigour of its
  check. Write the check into the description, or say plainly that none exists.
- **Fund verification as load-bearing.** When describing a program or a proposal, say what
  happens to the incentive if the checking budget thins in a lean year.
- **Trace the outcome to the beneficiary.** Who bears the cost, who takes the return, and over
  what horizon. Three plain sentences will usually do it.

### From TAME -- the discipline, carried into prose

TAME governs code. Four of its habits translate directly, and they are what make analysis
trustworthy.

- **Bound every claim.** A claim without a scope is an unbounded allocation. Write *"in this
  sector, over this period, under these assumptions."* Name the ceiling before the number.
- **State invariants before you lean on them.** Put your assumptions above the argument that
  rests on them, so a reader can check the footing before following you up.
- **Explicit widths.** Every figure carries its unit, its date, and where it came from. A number
  without those three is a rumour with a decimal point.
- **A share carries a fourth thing: its denominator.** *Forty percent of what?* is the question a
  reader cannot ask of a percentage, because the denominator is the one part a share does not
  show. Name it in the sentence. Measured `20260824.170904`: one reading of the same 1,891 modules
  gave a median of **14** against every comment line and **53** against ordinary comments alone --
  same data, same day, opposite conclusions, because half of all comments were declaration docs
  with no stake in the share they were diluting. Where two denominators are defensible, give both.
- **Say why.** Beside every surprising choice, threshold, or projection, one sentence on the
  reason. This is TAME's own rule and the single highest-value habit in the whole guide.
- **Safety first, performance second, joy third** becomes **honesty first, brevity second,
  delight third.** When they pull against each other, that order settles it.

## The three settings

One style, one dial. The setting follows the reader, and every setting keeps every inheritance
above.

**Door** -- READMEs, foundations, onboarding, anything a stranger meets first. Warmest and
plainest. Lead with what the reader gets. At most one cross-reference per paragraph, and every
coined term explained on sight. Negative framing at or under 20% of sentences.

**Field** -- technical documentation, analysis, forecasting, ecosystem description, design
essays. Measured and readable together. Assumptions stated up front, figures carrying units and
dates, and prose a curious non-specialist can follow. Negative framing at or under 30%.

**Meter** -- ledger rows, witness headers, scan comments, commit bodies. Exact, terse, and
indexed by what failed and what caught it. Refusal-first is correct here, because refusal is the
subject. No ceiling on negation; it is the content.

A document names its setting when the choice would surprise a reader. Most know their own.

## Writing analysis, projections, and forecasts

This is where the style earns its name, and where a careless page does the most damage.

### Separate the three kinds of claim, visibly

Every sentence in an analysis is one of three things, and a reader deserves to know which:

- **Observation** -- what is measurably so, with its unit, date, and source.
- **Inference** -- what follows from the observation, and by what reasoning.
- **Projection** -- what may come, over what horizon, under what conditions.

Keep them in separate sentences, and prefer separate paragraphs or separate sections. A page that
blends them reads as confident and teaches almost nothing, because a reader cannot tell which
part to check.

### Every projection carries four things

Write them plainly, in the prose, rather than in a footnote:

1. **The horizon.** By when.
2. **The assumptions.** What has to stay true for this to hold.
3. **The falsifier.** What observation would show this to be wrong. A forecast nobody could
   disprove is a mood.
4. **The confidence, in plain words.** *Likely*, *plausible*, *a live possibility*. Say the word
   and mean it.

The honest posture to aim for sounds like this: **I think this is right, I would like to be
right, and here is what would show me otherwise.** Conviction and falsifiability travel well
together, and a reader trusts a writer who supplies both.

### Base rates before narratives

Give the ordinary rate of the thing before the story about this instance. A sector's typical
margin, a category's usual timeline, the share of ventures in this shape that reach the outcome
being described. The narrative then does its proper work, which is explaining a departure from
the base rate rather than standing in for one.

### Magnitudes, with their dates

Write **"roughly a fifth of the market as of 2026"** rather than **"a large share."** Where a
figure is an estimate, say so and say whose. Where a figure is unknown, say that too; a named
gap is worth more to a reader than a confident adjective.

### Distinguish structural from cyclical

Say whether a trend rests on something durable -- a cost curve, a demographic, a regulation, a
physical property of a material -- or on a condition that turns. This one distinction carries
more forecasting weight than any other single sentence in a page of analysis.

### Name the reward, and follow the money

Civic Style, applied to a market rather than a policy. Who is paid, for what, by whom, and does
that payment point at the outcome everyone says they want. Thin margins reward speed; long
contracts reward durability. Say which pressure the actors in your description are actually
under.

## Describing an ecosystem

A sector, a movement, a supply chain, a standards body, a set of institutions.

- **Describe by role first, name second.** *A certification body that verifies growing practice*
  reads clearly to everyone; the acronym reads clearly to insiders. Give the role, then the name
  if the name helps. This is [the silo](SILO_TECHNIQUE.md) applied to market description, and it
  keeps the writing legible to a newcomer and portable past any one brand.
- **Name the actors' incentives, briefly.** A description that lists participants without their
  pressures explains nothing about what will happen next.
- **Say where the verification lives.** In an ecosystem built on claims -- ecological, ethical,
  nutritional, security -- the location and independence of the checking is the whole substance.
- **Give the scale, dated.** Ranges are welcome; precision theatre is not.
- **Name the constraint that actually binds.** Supply, capital, distribution, trust, or
  regulation. Most ecosystem descriptions list all five and rank none, which leaves the reader
  exactly where they started.
- **Be plain about your own position.** If the writer holds a stake, one sentence says so, early.

## Writing a design essay

The room for reasoning that outlives its code.

- **The durability test, applied before filing:** *would this still be worth reading if the code
  it describes were deleted?* Yes belongs in the essay room; no belongs in the round notes
  ([`../ORGANIZING.md`](../ORGANIZING.md)).
- **Open on the question**, rather than on the answer or the history. A reader who holds the
  question can follow any amount of reasoning.
- **Give the alternatives their best case.** An essay that describes the rejected option weakly
  has proven nothing, and a later reader will re-litigate it from scratch.
- **State the trade-off you accepted**, and what it costs. Every real design decision has a bill.
- **Mark proven and proposed** as [Two Rooms](TWO_ROOMS.md) asks, so a reader always knows which
  they are holding.

## Code comments

The dial runs through the code too, and the settings are the same.

**Meter, at a bound or an assert.** TAME already asks for the reason beside the constant, and the
reason is usually a failure that was designed away. Naming it plainly is exactly right.

```rye
// invariant: a caravan starts at most this many children, because a supervisor that
// can spawn without a ceiling is the failure it exists to prevent.
const max_children: u32 = 64;
```

**Door, at the head of a module.** A reader arriving here holds no context, and the opening
comment answers *what is this for*.

```rye
// Caravan -- the group that travels together and arrives together. It starts every
// other part of the system in the right order, watches each one, and brings back
// anything that stumbles, all inside limits fixed before it begins.
```

Keaton's reading of this tree's code -- *"kind of an obscure assembly"* -- is the same finding one
layer down. A module whose every comment sits at Meter is precise and hard to enter, and precision
a reader cannot reach is worth less than it looks. The standing aim is state-of-the-art code
explained in common English abstractions, which asks both settings to do the work each is good at.

## Quality assurance -- the report card

*Seated `20260824.161948` on Keaton's word.*

The table above is true and it is also backwards. A writer told to hold *20% negative* is aiming at
a ceiling, which is a thing you stay under rather than a thing you reach for, and a reader handed
that number has been told what the page managed to avoid. Read the same measurement the other way
up -- **100 minus that share** -- and 20% becomes **80, a B**: a grade a person has known since
school and can aim at without translating.

That flip is the whole idea, and once it is made the grade generalizes past prose. A **report card**
is four readings of one artifact, each 0 to 100, meaned into one grade and one letter. It reads a
document, a module's comments, or a design, and it answers one question in a form anyone can hold:
**how effectively and helpfully does this thing serve the work?**

### The four readings

| Reading | Asks | How it is taken |
|---|---|---|
| **Register** | Does it lead with what is? | **Counted.** 100 minus the share of sentences carrying a negative -- the same reading the register meter gates, lifted from that scan rather than spelled beside it. |
| **Reach** | Can the intended reader follow it? | **Counted.** Flesch-Kincaid grade and cross-references per hundred words, against the budget the setting carries. Ten points per whole unit over. An **index** is read as one -- see below. |
| **Truth** | Are its claims still honestly true? | **Half counted.** Counted: every relative path it cites, resolved as written, root-relative, or by the fold rule. A **placeholder shape** is an illustration -- see below. Judged: whether a behavioral claim still holds, which only reading or running can say. |
| **Service** | Does it help the work in front of us? | **Judged.** Read against the live card, `construction/ITINERARY.md`. The tool prints the inputs and stops there. |

**Truth is a gate rather than a quarter.** Below 60 the whole card reads **F**, whatever the other
three say. A page whose claims have gone false costs a reader more than an absent page, so
*honesty first* is written into the arithmetic rather than left to a habit.

### Service, in four questions

Service is the reading Keaton asked for by name, and it is judged because only a reader can take it.
Four questions, twenty-five points each, so the judgement is repeatable rather than a mood:

1. **Named.** Does a live line on the card, a standing law, or a roster reach this artifact?
2. **Reached.** Does someone arriving at the live work actually pass through it, rather than around it?
3. **Current.** Does it describe the tree as it stands today, rather than a season that has closed?
4. **Carried.** Which side does it serve -- the public projection `grain-os/grain`, the working
   repository `xy`, or both -- and is that the right side for what it holds?

The fourth question generalizes cleanly past this tree. Any organization has a published face and a
working one, and an artifact that serves neither is the one worth finding. An employee at Acme
Corporation reading this substitutes *the shipped product* for the first and *the working
repository* for the second, and the question is the same question.

### Two shapes the card reads rather than penalizes

Both were added on `20260825`, and both come from one finding: **a meter that instructs a repair
which would make the artifact worse is the thing to fix.** That sentence is the test to apply the
next time a reading looks wrong -- ask what the number is telling a writer to do, and whether doing
it would help anyone.

**An index is read as an index.** A cross-reference budget of one per hundred words is written for
prose. On an index the links *are* the content, so `docs/README.md` -- 10 words of prose carrying 5
links -- read 50 per hundred words and floored its Reach at zero. The card was telling a writer to
pad an index with prose until its denominator could carry its own rate.

So the card reads a page's own declaration, under **two conditions rather than one**:

1. The page **declares** itself an index in its **header** -- `**Depth:** routing`, or `**Kind:**`
   naming an index. Header only, meaning the block above the first `---` rule.
2. The page **measures** as one -- under **100 words** of prose, which is one full unit of the
   denominator the rate is expressed in.

Two conditions, because **a self-declared exemption is a door, and a door beside a wall makes the
wall a habit.** The pair discriminated on real data before the code was written: of the living
pages, four declare an index in their header, three carry 13, 13, and 10 words, and the fourth
carries 193 and stays graded at an A. When both conditions hold, the density is **reported** on the
card rather than scored, so a reader still sees it.

**A placeholder shape is an illustration.** `date/YYYYMMDD/name` is a *shape*, with letters standing
where digits would go, and [`../.claude/rules/stamp-and-name.md`](../.claude/rules/stamp-and-name.md)
seats that spelling and asks for it by name. `construction/CHECKPOINTS.md` sat at Truth 60, one
point off the F gate, for writing two of them correctly.

Only the two runs that law spells inside a path shape count -- `YYYYMMDD` and `HHMMSS` -- and zero
tracked paths in this tree carry either literally, so the reading can never swallow a real path. The
other half is what keeps it a reading rather than an escape: **a fabricated stamp -- digits naming
no file -- still costs twenty points**, which is exactly the failure the same law warns about. The
control plants both halves, and pointed at the old rule it refuses 13 of its 17 new readings.

### The scale

A plain school scale, and **no minus grades** -- so a writer reads a B and knows what to do.

| Score | Letter | | Score | Letter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 97-100 | **A+** | | 74-70 | **C** |
| 96-90 | **A** | | 69-65 | **D+** |
| 89-85 | **B+** | | 64-60 | **D** |
| 84-80 | **B** | | under 60 | **F** |
| 79-75 | **C+** | | | |

**B is the door, and B is 80 on purpose.** The Door setting's register ceiling of 20% negative
*is* 80 flipped, so the seated law and the new standard are one number rather than two that can
drift apart. A tree that spells a constant twice has already booked that lesson five times.

### Reading the three kinds of artifact

**A document** takes all four readings as written above.

**A module's comments** take the same dial the code section describes. Register and Reach run over
the comment text; the module's opening comment is read at **Door** and a comment beside a bound at
**Meter**. Service asks whether a reader arriving cold can say what the module is for. A module
whose every comment sits at Meter is precise and hard to enter, and that shows up here as a low
Service against a high Register, which is exactly the shape *"kind of an obscure assembly"* names.

**A design** puts most of its weight on Service and Truth: does this shape serve a live line, and
does what it claims still hold. Register and Reach read the essay carrying it. A design that reads
beautifully and serves nothing on the card is a B on two readings and a D on the two that matter.

### The tool, and what it refuses to invent

```

sh tools/fixtures/qa_report_card.sh --letter <0-100>
```

Without `--service` it prints what it counted, prints the inputs a judge needs -- living citers,

Held by [`../tools/q/qa_report_card_witness.rish`](../tools/q/qa_report_card_witness.rish), which
proves the scale at every one of its nine boundaries from both sides, proves the register reading is

**A grade is not a gate**, and the witness holds that line. The register share keeps its wall where
it already stands; the card aims a writer rather than refusing their work. What a low grade books is
a **molt**, and that discipline lives in
[`../.claude/rules/quality-assurance.md`](../.claude/rules/quality-assurance.md).

**What this does not prove** is that an A page is a good page. Four honest proxies, two of them
counted, and the second thing to read is always the artifact itself.

## Softening -- reach for the warmer word

Softening is the small, repeatable craft of the style: keep the meaning whole and let the sentence
land gently. It is the difference between a page a reader finishes and a page a reader survives.

**Softening keeps the claim exactly.** *"Sixteen documents read above target"* softens to *"sixteen
documents have room to come down."* Same sixteen, same target, warmer landing. A softening that
changes a number, hedges a finding, or blurs a boundary has stopped being softening and become
evasion, which this style has no room for.

### How to soften, in four moves

**Say the wanted thing.** Name what to reach for, and let the alternative go unmentioned or follow
after. *"Keep sentences to one aside"* carries further than *"don't stack dashes."*

**Turn the ban into a preference.** Where both halves help a reader, write them as a pair with the
good word first: *"reach for X rather than Y."* The reader learns the target and the trap in one
line, in that order.

**Let the fix carry the finding.** *"This page comes alive once the opening names who it is for"*
teaches everything *"the opening is unclear"* does, and hands over the repair as well.

**Lower the temperature, keep the level.** *"Worth another look"* does the work of *"badly broken"*
in nearly every case where a person will read it. Save the sharper word for the ledger, where
sharpness is the content.

### Words to reach for, and the ones they replace

The left column is the habit; the right is what it gently retires. Read it as a preference rather
than a wall -- any of these earns its place where it is genuinely the exact word, and the ledger's
**Meter** setting keeps all of them.

| Reach for | Rather than |
|---|---|
| worth another look, has room to grow | broken, bad, wrong, terrible, awful |
| reach for, prefer, favour, lean toward | avoid, don't, never, refuse to, steer clear of |
| holds, keeps, stays within | doesn't fail, cannot break, won't crash |
| has room to come down, is on its way | too high, over target, unacceptable |
| still to be written, arriving later | missing, absent, lacking, incomplete, nonexistent |
| a red, an error, a fault | a bug, a gremlin, a glitch |
| this teaches, this shows | this proves you wrong, this refutes |
| takes care, wants attention | hard, tricky, painful, a nightmare, a headache |
| an open question, a live choice | a problem, an issue, a blocker |
| ready when you are | blocked, stuck, stalled, dead in the water |
| grows, accretes, fills in | fixes, patches, repairs, cleans up |
| the reader may not have this yet | the reader is confused, obviously |
| one way to read it | the only sensible reading |
| we chose, we prefer | we refuse, we reject, we forbid |
| kept simple | dumbed down, watered down |
| plain | naive, simplistic, basic |

### Figures of speech we let go, and what we say instead

Many everyday sayings picture harm to animals. They are so common that most speakers never see the
image inside them, and once seen it is hard to unsee. This tree writes about tending and growing
things, so a gentler phrase is both kinder and truer to the subject.

| Reach for | Rather than |
|---|---|
| solve two things at once, one move for two ends | kill two birds with one stone |
| revisit a settled thing, keep at a closed question | beat a dead horse, flog a dead horse |
| more than one way to do it, several good paths | more than one way to skin a cat |
| earn a living, bring in the harvest | bring home the bacon |
| take it on directly, meet it head on | take the bull by the horns |
| a steady earner, a reliable source | a cash cow |
| a first trial, an early tester | a guinea pig |
| let a secret slip, share it early | let the cat out of the bag |
| one moment, give it a beat | hold your horses |
| what you have is worth more than what you hope for | a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush |
| spread the risk, keep more than one path open | don't put all your eggs in one basket |
| the heart of it, the substance | the meat of it |
| warm someone up, win goodwill | butter someone up |
| the thing everyone can see | the elephant in the room |
| a hard truth to swallow | a bitter pill, a tough pill |
| test it thoroughly, put it through its paces | beat it up, hammer on it |
| no use dwelling on what has passed | no use crying over spilt milk |
| tell it plainly | give it to them straight, no sugar-coating |
| a trial run, a first pass | a dry run through the meat grinder |

Where a saying carries no such image and simply works, keep it. This is a preference for kinder
pictures rather than a rule against idiom, and a warm familiar phrase is worth a great deal.

## Storytelling -- the voice at its best

A page that teaches is a page someone stayed with, and people stay for a story. Gauge Style asks
its writers to be genuinely good storytellers, in a particular key.

**The key, in one sentence:** an open-source, royalty-free, deeply helpful companion -- encouraging
and uplifting, and at the same time understated, cool, tranquil, calm, and collected. Warm without
being loud. Certain without being pushy. Glad you came, and entirely unhurried about it.

**Words for the register we are after:** quiet, steady, gentle, spacious, unhurried, grounded,
clear-eyed, generous, sober, luminous, patient, kind, exact, easy, plainspoken, companionable,
magical in the way a well-made thing is magical, and quietly fun.

**What good storytelling looks like here**

- **Open where the reader is.** Start from something they already hold, then walk one step. A field
  that feeds itself is a doorway into bounded allocation, and it works because everyone has stood
  in a garden.
- **One idea per paragraph, in order.** A story earns its next sentence. So does an explanation.
- **Let a concrete thing carry an abstract one.** A gauge on a wall, a caravan on a road, a sealed
  vessel. Pick the image once and stay with it rather than switching pictures midway.
- **Give the reader the small pleasure of arriving.** A paragraph that lands on the point, rather
  than trailing into a qualification, sends them into the next one gladly.
- **Keep the magic sober.** Wonder earns its place when the thing is genuinely wonderful and the
  sentence stays honest. A closed loop that feeds itself is remarkable; saying so plainly is the
  whole trick.
- **Be funny at your own expense, quietly, and rarely.** Never at the reader's, and never at the
  cost of clarity.
- **End where a reader can stand.** Foundations and front doors close on a benediction; ordinary
  and technical pieces close plainly. Either way, land somewhere.

**The register we let go**

Loud, obnoxious, breathless, salesy, or urgent. Clever for its own sake -- the pun that costs a
beat, the reference only insiders catch, the aphorism standing where a sentence belongs. Terse to
the point of coldness. Confusing by compression. Condescending, whether by over-explaining what a
reader plainly knows or by skipping what they plainly do not. Anything that would read as showing
off, which is the first rule of this guide arriving again in a different coat.

The test stays the same one: **would this feel generous, read aloud, to a friend who is smart,
curious, and new here?**

## Five conversion moves

Each is mechanical enough to apply on a first pass.

**Turn the refusal into the capability.** The fact survives the flip intact.

> Meter: *A check that cannot fail is not a check.*
> Door: *Every check here earns its place by catching a real mistake.*

**Give a coined maxim its plain meaning first, or drop it.** If the plain sentence says the whole
thing, keep only the plain sentence.

**Lead with the reader's stake, then the mechanism.** Invert the order rather than cutting the
detail. A stranger wants to know what they get; an engineer wants to know how. Both get served, in
that order.

**Spend one cross-reference per paragraph at Door.** Keep the handle a reader might follow today
and move the rest into the room that already holds them.

**Unstack the dashes.** One aside per sentence; the others become their own sentences. The prose
slows down in exactly the way Radiant asks for.

## What this guide does not do

It keeps Radiant Style whole, and Radiant remains the name for the warmest register and for the
voice this tree writes in. Gauge names the working style that carries measurements, and it borrows
Radiant's warmth rather than replacing it.

It softens no claim. Every number, path, stamp, and count is held exactly through any register
change, the same way a Radiant pass already holds them. A register may change; a claim never does.

It rewrites nothing that stands. Dated testimony keeps every word it wrote. Moving the living
documents into their right setting is a **standfast** awaiting Keaton's word, recorded at
`construction/REDS.md` row `%163`.

## Why the name

A plain word a newcomer grasps on first reading, and a true one. It also carries a debt worth
naming, recorded in full in the maintainer's gratitude room under *Galois, gauge theory, and the
sheaf*.

Three pieces of mathematics answer one question from three sides: *what part of a description is
actually about the world?* **Galois** answers with symmetry -- what survives every change you are
allowed to make is what the thing really is. **Gauge theory** answers with frame independence --
you may choose your units and your zero freely, and a real law gives the same answer whichever you
pick. **Sheaf theory** answers with local agreement -- patches that agree wherever they overlap
glue into one global truth, and a disagreement between patches is real information.

Read those three together and this guide's own rules fall out of them. The three settings are
frames, and the claim is what must survive all three: *a register may change; a claim never does*
is gauge invariance, written down before we knew its name. Every document here is a patch, and
where two pages describe the same thing they have to agree, which is why a contradiction between
documents counts as a defect rather than a wrinkle. And *ask what must survive this rewrite* is
the Galois habit used as an editing tool.

None of that mathematics is imported, claimed, or needed to use this guide. A reader needs no word
from it, which is the point. This writing measures and reports
exactly, and that is worth keeping. Giving it its own name lets Radiant mean what it says again,
and lets a gauge be excellent at what a gauge is for -- in the hands of someone glad you came to
read it.
