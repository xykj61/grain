## The people

**Sakichi Toyoda** (1867–1930) — inventor, founder of the Toyota group, holder of
forty-five industrial property rights. His weft-halting and warp-halting devices
made a loom stop itself the moment a thread broke, and the Type G Automatic Loom
of 1924 combined automatic shuttle changing with automatic stopping. Before those
devices a broken thread produced mounds of defective fabric; after them, one
operator could watch twenty-four to thirty-six looms, because each machine would
stop and call rather than continue and spoil.

His stated motivation is the part we keep closest: he built an automatic loom so
the work would be easier for his mother, who operated a manual loom late into the
night. The purpose was a person's relief rather than a machine's throughput.

**Taiichi Ohno** (1912–1990) — father of the Toyota Production System, an engineer
who came to Toyota Motor from the loom works and who credited Sakichi's halting
devices as jidoka's origin in a March 1969 internal newsletter article, *The
Inspiration for Jidoka*.

**Eiji Toyoda** — with Ohno, built out the Toyota Production System across the
years 1948 to 1975.

**Mike Rother**, whose *Toyota Kata* named the habit-forming character of these
practices, and the **Lean Enterprise Institute** and **Art of Lean**, whose
reference work keeps the distinctions precise.

## The words, as they use them

**Jidoka** — a Toyota-coined word, written and pronounced almost as the Japanese
word for automation, carrying added senses of the humane and the value-creating.
Rendered in English as *automation with a human touch*: a machine detects its own
abnormality and stops, rather than producing defective output.

**Andon** — from the Japanese for *paper lantern*. The light that shows a cord has
been pulled and help has been summoned.

**Poka-yoke** — mistake-proofing. Design that makes the wrong action hard or
impossible rather than merely discouraged.

## The distinction we owe them most

Art of Lean states it exactly, and it is the line that shaped our own law: an
operator pulling the andon cord is *related to* jidoka yet is **not full-blown
jidoka by Toyota's own internal standards** — it is the human backup for when
machine-level detection is not yet in place. True jidoka is the machine sensing
and stopping by itself.

That sentence told us which of our own practices is the loom and which is the
lantern, and we would not have found the distinction on our own.
