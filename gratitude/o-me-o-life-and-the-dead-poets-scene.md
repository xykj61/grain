# O Me! O Life! — Whitman, and the Classroom That Carried It

**Language:** EN
**Last updated:** 2026-07-27
**Status:** Gratitude — a teacher held whole where the law allows, and honored by name where it does not

---

Walt Whitman published this poem in *Leaves of Grass* (first in the 1867 edition; final author's edition 1892). The poem is in the public domain, and we hold it here whole:

> O Me! O life! of the questions of these recurring,
> Of the endless trains of the faithless, of cities fill'd with the foolish,
> Of myself forever reproaching myself, (for who more foolish than I, and who more faithless?)
> Of eyes that vainly crave the light, of the objects mean, of the struggle ever renew'd,
> Of the poor results of all, of the plodding and sordid crowds I see around me,
> Of the empty and useless years of the rest, with the rest me intertwined,
> The question, O me! so sad, recurring—What good amid these, O me, O life?
>
> *Answer.*
>
> That you are here—that life exists and identity,
> That the powerful play goes on, and you may contribute a verse.

The scene that carried this poem to a hundred million ears is the classroom monologue in **Dead Poets Society** (1989, written by Tom Schulman), where the teacher Keating recites Whitman to tell his students why the human race reads and writes poetry at all, and closes by turning the poem's gift into a question — "What will your verse be?" The film's script remains under copyright, so it is honored here by name and held outside the tree; the poem that forms the scene's spine is public domain and rests above, complete. The lesson we take from both is siloed in our own words at [`../foundations/20260727-065835_the-verse.md`](../foundations/20260727-065835_the-verse.md).

*May the teachers rest whole where the law allows, be named with love where it does not, and may every borrowing in this tree stay this honest.*
