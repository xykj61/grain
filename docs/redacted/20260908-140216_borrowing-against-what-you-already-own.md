# Borrowing against what you already own

If you own something that earns -- shares, a share of a business, land that pays -- there comes a day
when you need money and selling looks like the only door. Another door usually stands beside it.
This page is about that second door -- when it serves you better, and when it serves you worse.

## The test, in one line

**Borrowing beats selling while what you keep earns more than the loan costs.**

That is the whole comparison, and everything below is a term in it. Sell, and the future returns on what you sold go with it.
Borrow, and you keep those returns and pay a rate for the privilege. The larger number wins.

## What each term does

**The rate you pay** moves with a published benchmark plus a margin your lender sets. The benchmark
is public and you can read it yourself on the day you decide; the margin is where lenders differ,
often by more than you would expect for the same collateral.

**What you keep earning** is the honest hard part, because it is a *forecast* where the rate is a
*fact*. Give it a range rather than a number, and see whether the comparison still holds at the
bottom of that range. A comparison that holds only at the top of the range has found a hope
rather than an answer.

**Tax deferred stays owed, and deferral still helps.** Money that keeps working for you this year
earns for you this year. The cushion is small in one year and grows with the horizon; over a handful of years it can
be worth a fraction of a percent on the whole comparison. Small, real, and a supporting term rather than the reason.

**The structural term is the one to ask a professional about.** In some places, in some
circumstances, an obligation carried until death is treated very differently from one settled during
life. That single question can change the answer more than every rate you compared -- and it is
exactly the kind of question that depends on where you live, what you own, and what year it is.

## The finding that surprised the room

**The cheapest capital is sometimes selling the right piece, and you cannot know until you look.**

Anything you hold was bought at a price. Sell a piece bought near today's price and you realise
almost no gain, so the tax is small; sell a piece bought long ago and cheaply, and the tax can be
large. These can sit side by side in the same account under one name.

So the first move is to get the itemised record of what you paid for each piece, **before comparing a
single lender**. A sale costing a percent or two in tax beats a loan at any rate, and that option
stays invisible until you hold the list.

Method, stated plainly: *read your own position before you price anyone else's offer.*

## Where lenders differ, and the trap inside a discount

Rates for the same collateral vary widely across kinds of lender. The pattern worth carrying:

**The cheapest advertised rate often carries a condition that excludes you.** A discount offered for
holding other business with the lender may be written to exclude organisations, so an individual
qualifies and the same person's company does not. A venue with an attractive rate may open only the
kinds of account you do not need. And a quoted range from an aggregator becomes a quote only when it
arrives in writing.

**Read what the loan forbids alongside what it costs.** Many loans secured against investments carry
language barring the proceeds from buying more investments. If what you plan to buy is itself a
security -- and a share of a private company or a stake in a partnership usually is -- then a plan
that looks like one question is two, and the answer may route part of it to a different desk
entirely. Ask in writing, and keep the answer.

## Sizing, without arithmetic you cannot check

Borrow against a fraction of what you hold, not most of it, and decide the fraction by asking a
survivable question: **how far would the value have to fall before the lender demands repayment?**

At a modest fraction, that fall is deep -- deeper than most historical declines. At a large fraction,
an ordinary bad year reaches you. The number you choose is a statement about which of those you can
live through, and it is worth writing that sentence down before the money arrives.

## Keep the lanes separate from day one

If you borrow once and spend it on several different things, give each its own draw, its own
receiving account, and its own line in your books **from the first day**. Different uses are often
treated differently for tax, and reconstructing which dollar went where, a year later, is work that
goes badly for everyone who attempts it.

## Where this method stops

It compares a rate you can read against a return you must forecast, so **it is exactly as good as
the forecast**, and a forecast remains a forecast. Whether to own the thing at all is a separate
question, and the structural one about how obligations are treated where you live belongs to a
professional who knows your jurisdiction and your circumstances.

**This is a method rather than advice** -- a way to arrange your own question before you bring it to
someone qualified to answer it.

## What to hold onto

The comparison is simple and the inputs are not. Get the itemised record first, give your forecast a
range and test the bottom of it, read what the loan forbids as carefully as what it costs, size by
what you could live through, and separate the lanes on the first day.

*May what you have built keep working for you, and may you never have to sell the wrong piece of it.*
