# Lesson 1: Brahman, Paramatman, Bhagavan, and Prema-Bhakti in Three Arabics

**Question this lesson answers:** what is the Arabic equivalent of Sanskrit *prema-bhakti* toward
*Bhagavan*, *Paramatman*, and *Brahman* -- in everyday spoken Arabic, and how does that everyday
word differ from the word Classical Quranic Arabic uses, and from the word Abu Hamid al-Ghazali or
Ibn 'Arabi built for the same idea?

**The short answer, stated first:** there is no single Arabic word for any of the four Sanskrit
terms. There are three, because Arabic keeps three working registers side by side, and each
register built its own vocabulary for a different job. Reading a Sufi philosophical term back into
everyday speech is like reading *sat-cit-ananda* into a village blessing -- technically related,
practically a different sentence.

---

## Part 1: Why three registers, not one word

Arabic is a **diglossic** language, meaning two (here, three) forms live side by side without
either replacing the other:

1. **Everyday spoken Arabic, *al-'Ammiyya*.** No case endings, no dual verb conjugations, no
   distinct subjunctive or jussive mood -- and no separate word for "the impersonal Absolute
   underlying everything." A shopkeeper in Cairo, Damascus, or Dubai reaches for **Allah** and
   stops there, whatever the deeper question.
2. **Classical Quranic Arabic, *Fusha al-Turath*.** The revealed text's own fixed register, one
   which every spoken dialect has drifted away from over roughly fourteen centuries -- full case
   endings (*i'rab*), verbal mood, dual forms, and a phonology none of today's spoken varieties
   fully preserves.
3. **The technical Arabic of the philosophers and mystics.** Al-Ghazali (a jurist and theologian
   who also engaged deeply with philosophy) and Ibn 'Arabi (the mystic called *al-Shaykh al-Akbar*,
   "the Greatest Master") each needed to name distinctions the Quran's own vocabulary gestures at
   but does not spell out as a system. They built new compound terms out of ordinary roots, the
   same way a Sanskrit commentator builds *tattva* out of *tat* ("that").

So a question like "what is the Arabic for Paramatman?" really asks three separate questions. Below,
each Sanskrit term gets one answer per register.

---

## Part 2: Brahman -- the unconditioned absolute ground

### In Arabic script

- Everyday ('Ammiyya): اللَّه
- Classical Quranic (Fusha al-Turath): الْحَقّ -- and, more precisely for "unconditioned, needing
  nothing": الصَّمَد
- Al-Ghazali's technical register: نُورُ الْأَنْوَار -- and, drawing the vocabulary he shares with
  the philosophers he both studied and critiqued: وَاجِبُ الْوُجُود
- Ibn 'Arabi's technical register: الذَّاتُ الْمُطْلَقَة -- and, one level more precise, the state
  prior to any attribute at all: الْأَحَدِيَّة

### In transliteration and English

| Register | Arabic, transliterated | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday | *Allah* | God -- the one word, unqualified |
| Classical Quranic | *al-Haqq* | "The Real," one of the ninety-nine names |
| Classical Quranic | *as-Samad* | "The Eternally Self-Sufficient," Qur'an 112:2, the closest single Quranic word to "unconditioned absolute" |
| Al-Ghazali | *Nur al-Anwar* | "The Light of Lights," from his *Mishkat al-Anwar* ("The Niche of Lights") |
| Al-Ghazali (shared with the philosophers) | *Wajib al-Wujud* | "The Necessary Being" -- Avicennan vocabulary Ghazali engages closely, both against and with |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-Dhat al-Mutlaqah* | "The Absolute Essence" |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-Ahadiyyah* | "The station of pure, non-dual Oneness," prior to any Name or Attribute -- the level Ibn 'Arabi's school holds the Prophet Muhammad alone fully realized among human beings |

**The subtlety.** Everyday Arabic never separates "God as unconditioned absolute" from "God as the
one you pray to" -- both are simply *Allah*. The Qur'an's own *as-Samad* gestures at the same idea
Brahman names, in one dense verse (112:1-4, *Surat al-Ikhlas*), without building it into a system.
Ibn 'Arabi's *al-Ahadiyyah* is the one term here doing genuinely *systematic* philosophical work
comparable to Brahman -- naming a level of reality prior to and beneath every named attribute,
inside a worked-out metaphysics of levels (*hadarat*). Reading *al-Ahadiyyah* as "the Arabic word for
Brahman" is fair as a teaching bridge; reading it as what an ordinary reciter of *as-Samad* has in
mind is not.

## Part 3: Paramatman -- the indwelling witness, closer than the self

### In Arabic script

- Everyday ('Ammiyya): اللَّه فِي قَلْبِي -- and, addressed directly: رَبِّي مَعَايَ
- Classical Quranic (Fusha al-Turath): الرَّقِيب -- and, from the verse that names the relation
  most directly: أَقْرَبُ إِلَيْهِ مِنْ حَبْلِ الْوَرِيد
- Al-Ghazali's technical register: مَعْرِفَةُ الْقَلْب -- شَاهِدُ الْقَلْب
- Ibn 'Arabi's technical register: الْوُجُودُ السَّارِي -- الْمَعِيَّة

### In transliteration and English

| Register | Arabic, transliterated | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday | *Allah fi qalbi* | "God is in my heart" -- a devotional phrase, not a doctrine |
| Everyday | *Rabbi ma'aya* | "My Lord is with me" |
| Classical Quranic | *ar-Raqib* | "The Ever-Watchful," one of the ninety-nine names |
| Classical Quranic | *aqrab ilayhi min habl al-warid* | "closer to him than his own jugular vein" -- Qur'an 50:16, the verse this whole idea is anchored to |
| Al-Ghazali | *ma'rifat al-qalb* | "the knowing of the heart" -- the heart, in the *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, as the organ that actually perceives the Divine, when purified |
| Al-Ghazali | *shahid al-qalb* | "the witness of the heart" -- a fair paraphrase of Ghazali's teaching on the heart's inner witnessing, rather than a single fixed phrase quoted verbatim from one page |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-Wujud al-Sari* | "the Pervading Being" -- Being that runs through every existent thing without becoming that thing |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-Ma'iyyah* | "the withness" -- from Qur'an 57:4, *huwa ma'akum ayna ma kuntum*, "He is with you wherever you are" |

**The subtlety.** *Ar-Raqib* and Qur'an 50:16 are the Quran's own load-bearing verses for "closer
than the self" -- no later school invented the idea, only the systematic name for it. What Ghazali
and Ibn 'Arabi add is not a new claim but a new *precision*: Ghazali asks how the heart comes to
perceive this closeness (a psychology and an ethics of purification), and Ibn 'Arabi asks what kind
of being this closeness actually is (a metaphysics of pervading presence). Everyday speech keeps
only the devotional residue -- *Allah fi qalbi* -- with neither the verse's exact wording nor either
scholar's technical apparatus behind it, and that is not a loss; it is the same idea carried at the
register where it is actually lived.

## Part 4: Bhagavan -- the supreme personal deity, possessor of infinite names

### In Arabic script

- Everyday ('Ammiyya): رَبِّنَا -- يَا رَبّ
- Classical Quranic (Fusha al-Turath): الرَّبّ -- ذُو الْأَسْمَاءِ الْحُسْنَى
- Al-Ghazali's technical register: الْمَحْبُوبُ الْحَقِيقِيّ -- الْمَعْبُودُ بِالْحَقّ
- Ibn 'Arabi's technical register: رَبُّ الِاعْتِقَاد -- الْإِلٰهُ الْمُعْتَقَد

### In transliteration and English

| Register | Arabic, transliterated | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday | *Rabbina* | "Our Lord" -- the plain, warm, universal address |
| Everyday | *Ya Rabb* | "O Lord" -- the everyday call, used across every dialect and every register above it too |
| Classical Quranic | *ar-Rabb* | "The Lord" -- Nurturer, Sustainer, the party to a covenant, addressed directly and constantly throughout the Qur'an |
| Classical Quranic | *Dhu-l-Asma al-Husna* | "Possessor of the Most Beautiful Names," Qur'an 7:180 -- the Quran's own direct parallel to Bhagavan as the one who holds infinite auspicious attributes |
| Al-Ghazali | *al-Mahbub al-Haqiqi* | "the True Beloved" -- from the *Ihya's* Book of Love |
| Al-Ghazali | *al-Ma'bud bi-l-Haqq* | "the One rightly worshipped" |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *Rabb al-i'tiqad* | "the Lord of one's own belief" |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-Ilah al-Mu'taqad* | "the god held in belief" -- Ibn 'Arabi's own famous teaching, from the *Fusus al-Hikam*'s chapter on Noah, that each heart knows the Haqq only in the shape its own belief gives it, while the Haqq itself exceeds every such shape |

**The subtlety.** *Ar-Rabb* and *Dhu-l-Asma al-Husna* are exactly Bhagavan's own sense: a personal
Lord, in relationship, holding infinite good attributes by name. Everyday Arabic keeps the
relationship (*Rabbina*, *Ya Rabb*) and drops the theology of attributes -- a person saying *Ya Rabb*
before an exam is not thinking about the ninety-nine names, any more than someone saying "thank God"
is doing systematic theology. Ibn 'Arabi's *al-Ilah al-Mu'taqad* is the sharpest and most
often-quoted line in this whole lesson, and it cuts a different way than Bhagavan does: it is a
teaching about the *limits* of any one person's image of the personal God, rather than a naming of
the personal God itself. It belongs here as the register's own answer to "the God one relates to,"
with that one qualification kept visible.

## Part 5: Prema-Bhakti -- loving devotion

### In Arabic script

- Everyday ('Ammiyya): حُبّ رَبِّنَا -- قَلْبِي مِتْعَلِّق بِرَبِّي
- Classical Quranic (Fusha al-Turath): يُحِبُّهُمْ وَيُحِبُّونَه -- مَحَبَّة -- عِبَادَة
- Al-Ghazali's technical register: الشَّوْق -- الْأُنْس -- الرِّضَا
- Ibn 'Arabi's technical register: الْعِشْق -- الْمُحِبّ وَالْمَحْبُوبُ وَالْحُبّ

### In transliteration and English

| Register | Arabic, transliterated | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday | *hubb Rabbina* | "love of our Lord" -- the plain, ordinary phrase |
| Everyday | *qalbi mit'allaq bi-Rabbi* | "my heart is attached to my Lord" -- popular devotional speech, common in religious song |
| Classical Quranic | *yuhibbuhum wa-yuhibbunahu* | "He loves them and they love Him," Qur'an 5:54 -- the Quran's own explicit statement of mutual, reciprocal love |
| Classical Quranic | *mahabbah* | "love" -- from the root H-B-B, the ordinary Quranic word for love, of every kind |
| Classical Quranic | *'ibadah* | "worship, loving servitude" -- from the root '-B-D, establishing the Creator-created relationship this love happens inside of |
| Al-Ghazali | *ash-shawq* | "yearning" -- from Book 36 of the *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, titled *Kitab al-Mahabbah wa-l-Shawq wa-l-Uns wa-l-Rida* ("The Book of Love, Yearning, Intimacy, and Contentment") |
| Al-Ghazali | *al-uns* | "intimate joy, closeness" -- the same book's second named state, the felt companionship that follows yearning |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-'ishq* | "consuming, passionate love" -- the subject of Ibn 'Arabi's own poetry collection, *Tarjuman al-Ashwaq* ("The Interpreter of Desires") |
| Ibn 'Arabi | *al-Muhibb, al-Mahbub, al-Hubb* | "the Lover, the Beloved, and Love itself" -- Ibn 'Arabi's teaching that at the furthest reach of *'ishq*, the three distinct terms of the relationship dissolve into one single reality |

**The subtlety, and the closest match in this whole lesson.** *Prema-bhakti* -- love as the very
essence and goal of devotion, not merely its ethical precondition -- has its nearest Arabic
neighbor in exactly the pair Ghazali names as a dedicated book of the *Ihya*: *shawq* and *uns*,
yearning reaching toward intimate closeness. Ibn 'Arabi's *'ishq* goes one step further than Ghazali
is willing to go in his own more restrained juristic register, toward a love so total that lover,
beloved, and love stop being countable as three things. The Qur'an's own *mahabbah* (5:54) is the
seed both scholars grew from, stated as a plain, mutual, two-way fact rather than developed into a
system. Everyday Arabic keeps the warmth of all three, in one unpretentious sentence, with no
citation attached -- and that is exactly right for what everyday speech is for.

---

## Closing note

None of the four terms in the tables above is "wrong." Each is the honest vocabulary its own
register actually reaches for, built to do that register's own job -- a shopkeeper's blessing, a
revealed verse, a jurist's book of the heart, a mystic's poem. Placed side by side rather than
collapsed into one, they show something Sanskrit and Arabic share without either language having
to give anything up: that the language of ordinary devotion, the language of scripture, and the
language of the philosophers who spent a lifetime staring at scripture, are three different and
equally necessary tools, in both traditions alike.

May this room's tables serve as a true bridge rather than a false equation -- naming what each
register actually says, in its own right, so a reader crossing from one sacred language to another
arrives having learned two things clearly instead of blurring both into one.
