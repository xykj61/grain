# Lesson 2: The Twelve Zodiac Signs and the Sky's Craft in Three Arabics

**Question this lesson answers:** what are the Arabic names for the twelve zodiac signs, and for
the other working words of the reading craft -- the planets, the lunar mansions, the houses, the
ascendant, dignity and fall, and the two nodes -- in everyday spoken Arabic, in Classical Quranic
Arabic, and in the technical Arabic the astronomers built?

**The short answer, stated first:** the twelve sign names are the same ordinary nouns in every
register -- *al-Hamal* "the lamb," *ath-Thawr* "the bull," *al-Jawza'* "the twins" -- because Arabic
named its zodiac by translating the Greek signs into plain Arabic words any speaker already knows.
The three-register split re-emerges the moment the craft goes deeper: the sun and moon are Quranic
words, the five planets carry pre-Islamic technical names the Quran never uses, and the lunar
mansions (*manazil al-qamar*) are the Arabic tradition's own counterpart to the nakshatras, built
in the technical register alone.

---

## Part 1: The word for "sign" itself -- *burj*, a Quranic word put to astronomical work

The Arabic word for a zodiac sign is **برج** (*burj*, plural **بروج** *buruj*). Its root meaning is
"tower, fortress, or the visible rising of a thing," and it is a Quranic word before it is an
astronomical one: Qur'an 85:1 opens *wa-s-sama'i dhati-l-buruj* -- "By the sky, possessor of the
towers" -- where the *buruj* are the constellations, the fixed "towers" the sky is built of.

The technical register then borrowed this Quranic word and gave it a precise job: a *burj* became
one of the twelve equal divisions of the ecliptic, the "sign" a planet stands in. So the three
registers line up unusually cleanly here:

| Register | Word | What it means there |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday ('Ammiyya) | *burj* | "your sign" -- *shu burjak?* "what's your sign?", the ordinary horoscope question |
| Classical Quranic (Fusha al-Turath) | *burj* | "tower, constellation" -- Qur'an 85:1, the sky's fixed towers |
| Technical (the astronomers) | *burj* | "zodiac sign" -- one of twelve equal ecliptic divisions |

The same word, three jobs, and the technical job grew directly out of the Quranic one -- a rare case
where the three registers share one term rather than building three.

---

## Part 2: The twelve signs, named by ordinary nouns

Arabic received its zodiac from the Greek tradition (through Syriac and Pahlavi intermediaries), and
translated each sign's image into a plain Arabic noun. The result is that a sign's name is not a
technical coinage but an everyday word -- "the lion," "the scales," "the fish" -- which is why the
sign names do not split by register the way the devotional terms of Lesson 1 do.

### In Arabic script and transliteration

| # | English sign | Arabic | Transliteration | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Aries | الحمل | *al-Hamal* | the lamb, the ram |
| 2 | Taurus | الثور | *ath-Thawr* | the bull |
| 3 | Gemini | الجوزاء | *al-Jawza'* | the twins |
| 4 | Cancer | السرطان | *as-Saratan* | the crab |
| 5 | Leo | الأسد | *al-Asad* | the lion |
| 6 | Virgo | العذراء | *al-'Adhra'* | the virgin, the maiden |
| 7 | Libra | الميزان | *al-Mizan* | the balance, the scales |
| 8 | Scorpio | العقرب | *al-'Aqrab* | the scorpion |
| 9 | Sagittarius | القوس | *al-Qaws* | the bow |
| 10 | Capricorn | الجدي | *al-Jady* | the kid, the young goat |
| 11 | Aquarius | الدلو | *ad-Dalw* | the bucket, the water-bearer |
| 12 | Pisces | الحوت | *al-Hut* | the fish, the whale |

**The subtlety.** Eleven of the twelve are transparent translations of the Greek image, and a
speaker of Arabic reads them the way an English speaker reads "the Ram" or "the Scales" -- as
pictures, not as technical jargon. The one that carries a second, older layer is **الجوزاء**
(*al-Jawza'*, Gemini): the word is also the classical Arabic name for the constellation Orion, and
its root (*j-w-z*, "to pass through, to be in the middle") points at the figure's belt, the middle
of the sky. So Gemini's Arabic name reaches back past the Greek "twins" to an older native
star-name, and the two senses -- "the twins" and "the middle one" -- sit side by side in the same
word.

## Part 3: The seven planets -- Quranic lights, and five pre-Islamic names

The Arabic word for "planet" is **كوكب** (*kawkab*, plural **كواكب** *kawakib*), a Quranic word for
a bright heavenly body. But the seven classical planets themselves split cleanly by register, and
the split is the lesson:

### In Arabic script and transliteration

| Planet | Arabic | Transliteration | Register and note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sun | الشمس | *ash-Shams* | Quranic -- one of the most frequent words in the text |
| Moon | القمر | *al-Qamar* | Quranic -- named throughout, and the subject of its own sura (54, *al-Qamar*) |
| Mercury | عطارد | *'Utarid* | Technical -- a pre-Islamic name, from the Babylonian scribe-god Nabu's tradition |
| Venus | الزهرة | *az-Zuhara* | Technical -- "the bright, blooming one," from a root meaning "to shine, to blossom" |
| Mars | المريخ | *al-Mirrikh* | Technical -- "the red, rust-colored one," from a root meaning "to be red" |
| Jupiter | المشتري | *al-Mushtari* | Technical -- "the buyer," a name whose origin the lexicographers themselves record as uncertain |
| Saturn | زحل | *Zuhal* | Technical -- "the one who withdraws, the distant one," from a root meaning "to be far, to lag" |

**The subtlety.** The Quran names the two lights -- *ash-Shams* and *al-Qamar* -- constantly, and
names the stars and the *buruj* as a class, but it never names the five planets individually. The
five planet-names are the technical register's own inheritance, carried into Arabic from the older
Mesopotamian and Greek astronomical tradition, and they are the words everyday Arabic still uses
today -- a person in Cairo or Damascus knows *al-Mirrikh* is Mars without ever having read a
treatise. So here the three registers do not collapse into one word, as they did for the signs, but
into two: the Quranic lights on one side, and the five technical names on the other, with everyday
speech borrowing from both.

## Part 4: The lunar mansions -- *manazil al-qamar*, the nakshatras' own counterpart

The reading craft this tree keeps divides the moon's path into twenty-seven **nakshatras**. The
Arabic tradition built a parallel system of its own: the **منازل القمر** (*manazil al-qamar*), "the
moon's resting-places," twenty-eight divisions of the moon's monthly path, each named for a star or
asterism the moon passes. The two systems are not the same -- twenty-seven against twenty-eight, and
different boundary stars -- but they answer the same question, and the Arabic names are worth
meeting on their own terms.

### The first twelve mansions, in Arabic script and transliteration

| # | Arabic | Transliteration | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | الشرطان | *ash-Sharatan* | the two signs (the horns of the Ram) |
| 2 | البطين | *al-Butayn* | the little belly |
| 3 | الثريا | *ath-Thurayya* | the Pleiades |
| 4 | الدبران | *ad-Dabaran* | the follower (Aldebaran) |
| 5 | الهقعة | *al-Haq'a* | the brand, the mark |
| 6 | الهنعة | *al-Han'a* | the brand |
| 7 | الذراع | *adh-Dhira'* | the forearm |
| 8 | النثرة | *an-Nathra* | the sprinkle |
| 9 | الطرف | *at-Tarf* | the glance, the eye |
| 10 | الجبهة | *al-Jabha* | the forehead |
| 11 | الزبرة | *az-Zubra* | the mane |
| 12 | الصرفة | *as-Sarfa* | the turning, the change |

**The subtlety.** These are the technical register's own coinages, and they are the one place in
this lesson where the Arabic tradition is not translating the Greeks but naming its own sky. The
names are concrete and bodily -- a belly, a forearm, a forehead, a mane -- the way the nakshatras
name a horse's head (Ashvini) or a hand (Hasta). The most famous of them, **الثريا** (*ath-Thurayya*,
the Pleiades), is a word every Arabic speaker knows from proverb and poetry, and it is the single
clearest bridge between the two systems: a star-cluster both traditions watched the moon pass, and
both gave a name of its own.

## Part 5: The rest of the craft -- ascendant, houses, dignity, and the two nodes

Four more working words round out the sky's vocabulary, and each splits by register in its own way.

### In Arabic script and transliteration

| Concept | Arabic | Transliteration | Literal gloss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ascendant | الطالع | *at-Tali'* | the rising one -- the degree rising on the eastern horizon at birth |
| House | بيت | *bayt* (pl. بيوت *buyut*) | house -- the twelve *buyut* are the chart's houses |
| Exaltation, dignity | شرف | *sharaf* | honor, nobility -- a planet in its exaltation stands in *sharaf* |
| Fall, debilitation | هبوط | *hubut* | descent, falling -- a planet in its fall stands in *hubut* |
| The north node (Rahu) | رأس التنين | *ra's at-tinnin* | the dragon's head |
| The south node (Ketu) | ذنب التنين | *dhanab at-tinnin* | the dragon's tail |

**The subtlety.** Two of these are worth pausing on. **الطالع** (*at-Tali'*, the ascendant) is a
technical term built from an ordinary root (*t-l-',* "to rise"), and it names the chart's hinge --
the degree rising at the moment of birth -- with a word that also means simply "the one coming up,"
so the technical and the everyday senses stay close. And **رأس التنين / ذنب التنين** ("the dragon's
head / the dragon's tail") is the one term this lesson shares almost word-for-word with the Indian
tradition: Rahu and Ketu are themselves the Sanskrit names for the same two nodes, and the
"dragon's head and tail" image traveled with the astronomy itself, so that Arabic, Greek, and
Sanskrit all point at the same two invisible points in the sky with the same picture.

---

## Closing note

None of the names above is a "translation" of a Sanskrit term, and this lesson does not offer them
as one. The twelve signs are Arabic's own rendering of a Greek sky; the five planets are Arabic's
own inheritance from an older astronomy; the lunar mansions are Arabic's own answer to a question
the nakshatras also answer. Placed side by side rather than collapsed, they show what the two
traditions share without either having to give anything up: that a sky can be divided and named in
more than one true way, and that the ordinary nouns of a language -- a lamb, a bull, a balance, a
fish -- are enough to carry a whole craft, in Arabic exactly as they are in Sanskrit.

May this room's tables serve as a true bridge rather than a false equation -- naming what each
register actually says, in its own right, so a reader crossing from one sky-language to another
arrives having learned two things clearly instead of blurring both into one.
