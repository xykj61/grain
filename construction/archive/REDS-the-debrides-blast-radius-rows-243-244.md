word, and the two things it broke were both instruments: the round-open procedure that assumes two
piers share an ancestor, and the privacy wall whose whole content is a list of names to rewrite.

**REDS %243 (`20260826.010645`) -- the round-open rota had no reading for a rewritten upstream, and the quiet path rests on a reflog.** *What went wrong:* the sync rota seated `20260825.210819` opens every round with `git pull --rebase xykj61 main`, and all fifteen of its sentences assume the two piers descend from a common ancestor. A **deep debride** is the one act licensed to break that assumption, and it was spent from the macOS bench on `20260825` -- a collaborator's name out of the tree, the logs, two filenames, and every commit, re-signed and force-pushed. This pier opened its next round holding two commits of its own and met an upstream of 3,469 commits against a local 3,467 with **no merge base at all**. Against that shape `git pull --rebase` has two behaviors rather than one, and which arrives turns on the **remote-tracking reflog** rather than on anything in the tree: with it, `--fork-point` finds the pre-rewrite position and replays only what the pier owns; without it the rebase replays **every** local commit onto a history that never held them, and each conflict is a chance to resolve toward the side still carrying the debrided name. Git expires that reflog at ninety days by default and a fresh clone has none, so the safe path is the one a pier cannot rely on having. *What caught it:* a dirty index. A lap had ended at `git add`, so the pull refused to start before it could do either thing -- REDS %223's third rung working as designed, for an entirely different reason than the one that saved this round. The shape was then read by hand and the two commits carried across with `git rebase --onto`, which is the repair the rota should have named. *What it taught:* **a procedure and the sanctioned exception to it belong in the same place, or the exception is discovered by whoever meets it.** The debride rule already names the bill -- every downstream re-clones or hard-resets -- and the rota, written one day before the debride was spent, never read that rule. The right outcome here arrived by accident, which is the part worth booking: nothing in the round-open would have caught the expensive path, and on this tree the expensive path is 3,467 replays. *Repaired:* [`../tools/fixtures/upstream_shape_scan.sh`](../../tools/fixtures/upstream_shape_scan.sh) reads HEAD against the ordering remote and names the shape -- `up_to_date`, `fast_forward`, `ahead`, `diverged`, `no_remote_ref`, or `rewritten` -- printing the merge base, the fork point or its absence, and the count a plain rebase would replay. Only `rewritten` refuses, and it refuses every lap until the pier rebases, since a pier on a disjoint history has nothing safe to do until it moves. [`../tools/u/upstream_shape_witness.rish`](../../tools/u/upstream_shape_witness.rish) proves it five ways on metal: the live shape; two real repositories under a real `filter-branch` rewrite with the reflog removed the way ninety days removes it, where **all 15 local commits queue for replay and the debrided name measures back on disk**; the same pen with the reflog intact, where **7 owned commits replay and the tree stays clean**; an ordinary divergence proven to pass as hard as the rewrite is proven to refuse; and an unfetched remote read as a machine fact rather than a tree red. *Surfaced, not crossed:* a debride announcing itself in the tree -- a `CHECKPOINTS.md` line naming the rewrite's stamp and the last shared commit subject, which a round-open could read -- changes how both piers record a custody act, so it waits for Keaton's word. Design: [`../active-designing/20260826-010645_the-rota-meets-the-debride.md`](../../active-designing/20260826-010645_the-rota-meets-the-debride.md).

**REDS %244 (`20260826.010645`) -- the blanket rewrite edited the guard whose whole content is a list of what to rewrite.** *What went wrong:* the `20260825` deep debride ran `--replace-text` over every commit, turning a collaborator's two identity strings into the pseudonym **DJINN** throughout the history. `tools/fixtures/sow_leak_scan.sh` holds the seed's privacy wall as one alternation of **77** maintainer strings -- names, prior names, handles, key fingerprints, contact forms -- and two of its entries *were* those strings. The rewrite reached inside them, so from that commit the wall hunted the public pseudonym, which is the one name the debride created **so that it could be shipped**. Twenty-two projected files failed a privacy check that had nothing to do with privacy, and the two entries were left as literal duplicates of each other. *What caught it:* `sow` red on this pier's cold roster, then splitting the alternation into its 77 parts and counting which fired: two entries, 22 files each. Reading the file would not have shown it -- both spellings look exactly like every other entry on a list of things to scrub. *What it taught:* **a blanket text rewrite has no exception for the tool that names the thing being rewritten.** The debride's own record shows the shape was half-known: `tools/fixtures/sow_scrub.sed` was re-spelled by hand with bracketed patterns in the same commit *so the guard would outlive the name*, and its sibling one directory over was left alone. A repair aimed at the file that was noticed rather than at the class is REDS %237's lesson in a second language. *Repaired:* the two `DJINN` entries removed, the wall reading `IDENT_CLEAN`, and a paragraph in the file naming why the pseudonym is deliberately absent, so nobody adds it back. **The defense one layer up was proven rather than assumed:** both debrided strings were taken from the pre-rewrite history and fed through `sow_scrub.sed`, which still rewrites both -- so a document reintroducing either is scrubbed at projection before the leak scan ever runs.
