## Why this file, and what belongs in it

Own-reds-immediately has been a house law for a long time, and it worked: reds were named the moment they were found, out loud, without excuse. What the law never said is **where**. So the ones that happened to land inside a witness header or a spec erratum survived, and the ones that happened in a sentence did not.

Three fields per row, because a red with fewer teaches nothing:

- **What went wrong** -- stated plainly, no softening.
- **What caught it** -- a compiler, a stopwatch, a guard, a second look. Never "I noticed."
- **What it taught** -- the transferable rule, which is the only part that outlives the incident.

A red enters this ledger when it is found. Rows are never edited or removed; a later correction accretes as a new row pointing back.

## What the pattern says

Read down the *what caught it* column and almost nothing was caught by thinking harder. A compiler, a stopwatch, a file-type check, a guard's first run, seven witnesses at once, a pack catching its own author. **The machine caught nineteen of twenty.**

That is not a complaint about judgment; it is the argument for the whole apparatus. Witnesses exist because the author is the last person able to see their own gap, and this column is twenty lines of evidence for that claim rather than one more assertion of it.

Read down the *what it taught* column and four rules recur: **measure before claiming**, **scope before shipping**, **fixture rather than remember**, and **narrow the objection**. Those four have paid for themselves repeatedly in a single sitting.

**REDS %195 (`20260824.121445`) -- thirteen pages declare how long they will stay, and nothing in the tree has ever read one of those declarations.** *What went wrong:* every page on the docs compression shelf carries a `**Ceiling:** <=N lines` header -- the page's own promise about its length, written by whoever made it and read by whoever arrives next. Measured this lap: **13 pages declare a ceiling, and not one of them was both readable and honoured.** Twelve wrote the single-glyph form of `<=`, which no parser in this tree reaches, so the declaration was invisible to tooling while reading perfectly to a person. The thirteenth, `docs/CRYPTO.md`, had its header rewritten into a paragraph of prose *narrating its own drift* -- `the declared <=300 lines stands at 307` -- which is a page describing a broken promise in the exact place a tool would look to check it. Underneath the unreadable declarations two pages stood over their number: `docs/README.md` at **43 lines against 40**, and `docs/CRYPTO.md` at **311 against 300**. *What caught it:* the previous lap's own recommend line, which named the reading twice and left it open both times, and then a five-line shell loop that printed each page's declaration beside its `wc -l`. The measurement took under a minute; the reading had stood open since `20260824.095920`. *What it taught, and it is the sharper half:* **a declaration a tool cannot read is indistinguishable from no declaration at all.** A ceiling gate alone would have reported this shelf clean, because a header it cannot parse is a page it never measures -- so the guard would have been green over twelve unmeasured promises and one broken one. The readable-form gate is therefore not a tidiness rule riding along beside the real check; it *is* the check, and the line-count gate is what it enables. This is REDS %83's ASCII lesson arriving in a new room: the glyph did not corrupt anything, it simply made a promise unreadable, which cost the same. *And a third reading, counted rather than hidden:* `docs/STOA.md` declares `<=80` in its **H1 title**, in the grade notation the shelf index uses, and stands at **166 lines**. Gating it would force a page split into the round that seated the guard, so it rides a ceiling that only ever falls -- the form may shrink and may not spread -- and it prints at every roster pass.
