Two gods, four faces, held together the way a cubist canvas holds two brothers on one
ground: the teaching as the classical room keeps it, the counts its page carries, the
habit those counts feed in a working tree, and the pair of words the brothers wear by day
and by night. Bhakti names the love the reading serves -- this page reads the star-gods as
devotion, and the pairing is its shape: the bright gift and the heavy gift, sons of one Sun.

## The teaching, kept whole

The classical page sets two neighboring stars beside each other and gives their gods as
brothers, both children of the Sun. The first star belongs to the shining twins, born all
at once while both their parents wore the form of horses, carrying light, beauty, and
speed from their first breath. The tradition holds them as the healers of the gods: they
could return youth to the old, give sight to the unseeing, and mend what seemed past
mending -- even lift away a weary head, set a fresh one gently in its place, and make the
body whole again. They also taught one of the kindest arts the page names: finding water
in a dry spell, digging wells, and bringing the streams to the fields. They loved to run
free, loved good company, and were never solemn -- the bright morning of things.

The story of their seat carries the star's two quiet lessons. Kept from the strengthening
nectar by a worried king who feared being outshone, the twins neither sulked nor forced
the door. They used the gifts they already had: traded their healing art to a wise keeper
of secrets in fair exchange for knowledge, healed their way around the threat hanging over
him, and restored an aged sage to radiant youth for the sake of his devoted young wife --
and the grateful sage helped them at last to their seat. The heart of it is that the
other gods liked them: gifts shown openly had gathered friends, and the friends spoke up.
So the twins' way is to find the good you carry, show it generously, and gather the
companions whose gifts complete your own.

The second star belongs to their brother, the fair judge. When the worried king took the
throne, the old power of complete, all-seeing fairness passed to this brother instead --
together with a faithful record-keeper who notices everything, and a cord for gently
drawing each soul to account. His name means discipline and restraint, and the page holds
that nearer his true nature than any darker title: he belongs to no team, plays no
favorites, and looks only at what was actually done, so that every action meets its honest
fruit and the good is protected. And his discipline always serves something tender -- the
small parental rule about a jacket on a cold day is love guarding something precious, and
so are his boundaries. The precious thing they protect is always the point.

## The habit it feeds

The two brothers translate into a working tree without any forcing, because the page has
already made them a method and passed a baton between them. The twins go first: a closed
door answers to the gift used generously in the open, the healing traded fairly, the well
dug where the field is dry, never to sulking or forcing. In a tree that is
work shipped small and visible, tooling that waters other people's gardens, proof shown
where companions can find it -- and the companions who then speak for the work are the
door opening from the other side. The judge receives the baton: the witness on metal that
looks only at what was actually done, the ledger that notices everything and favors no
one, the bound whose reason is written beside it so the restraint reads as care -- a guard
felt as a jacket on a cold day is a guard kept willingly. Bright gift first, heavy gift
holding: vitality gathers the hands, and discipline protects what they build.

## The pair it wears

By day the brothers wear **river**; by night, **tide** -- the water element's cardinal
seat in the fifteen-pair grid of `kyri/receipt.rye`, where the row comment reads *the
river cuts* and the palette note says the river opens into the tide. The why is the page's
own water. The twins taught the digging of wells and the bringing of streams to the
fields: that is the river's day, vitality moving outward, the bright morning cutting a
generous channel so dry ground can grow. And the night-word keeps Twilight's rule, calm
and never gloom. The tide is the judge: drawn by a quiet law, reaching every shore
without favorites, gently returning every water to account -- the cord felt as the moon's
own pull, restraint that carries rather than punishes. The seat is honestly this page's
own: the water row's dual seat is worn twice in this room already, the cardinal stood
open, and cardinal fits truer besides -- both brothers initiate, the twins the morning
and the judge the reckoning. Aim, then dissolve: the day-word carries the stream out to
the fields, and the night-word draws it home, keeping the fairness.
